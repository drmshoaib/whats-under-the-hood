What does fit() actually solve?

Dr. Muhammad Shoaib · Applied Mathematics & Data Science

The question behind fit()

A linear regression model can be fitted in one line:

from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression
model = LinearRegression().fit(X, y)

The API is deliberately simple. Underneath it sit several distinct mathematical objects: a convex optimisation problem, a projection in an $n$-dimensional vector space, a statistical estimator with a sampling distribution, and a numerical linear-algebra problem whose conditioning matters.

This lecture develops those pieces from first principles and then reconnects them to what a production library actually computes.

1. Model and least-squares formulation

Suppose we observe $n$ responses $y_i$ and $p$ predictors. With an intercept, define the augmented design matrix

$$ X=\begin{pmatrix} 1 & x_{11} & \cdots & x_{1p}\\ 1 & x_{21} & \cdots & x_{2p}\\ \vdots & \vdots & & \vdots\\ 1 & x_{n1} & \cdots & x_{np} \end{pmatrix}\in\mathbb R^{n\times(p+1)},\qquad y=\begin{pmatrix}y_1\\ \vdots\\ y_n\end{pmatrix},\qquad \beta=\begin{pmatrix}\beta_0\\ \vdots\\ \beta_p\end{pmatrix}. $$

The linear model is

$$y=X\beta+\varepsilon.$$

"Linear" here means linear in the parameters. A model such as

$$y=\beta_0+\beta_1x+\beta_2x^2+\varepsilon$$

is still a linear model because the coefficients enter linearly.

For a proposed coefficient vector $\beta$, the residual vector is

$$r(\beta)=y-X\beta.$$

Ordinary least squares chooses $\beta$ to minimise its squared Euclidean norm:

$$\hat\beta=\arg\min_\beta\;\|y-X\beta\|_2^2.$$

Equivalently,

$$\operatorname{RSS}(\beta)=\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-x_i^\top\beta)^2=(y-X\beta)^\top(y-X\beta).$$

This optimisation statement needs no probability model. OLS can be defined purely as the minimiser of a quadratic loss.

2. Why squared error appears: the Gaussian likelihood

Now add a probabilistic model. Suppose

$$\varepsilon\mid X\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2I_n),$$

so that each observation satisfies

$$y_i\mid x_i\sim\mathcal N(x_i^\top\beta,\sigma^2).$$

The conditional density is

$$p(y_i\mid x_i,\beta,\sigma^2)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\!\left[-\frac{(y_i-x_i^\top\beta)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right].$$

Assuming conditional independence, the likelihood is

$$L(\beta,\sigma^2)=\prod_{i=1}^n p(y_i\mid x_i,\beta,\sigma^2).$$

Taking logarithms,

$$ \ell(\beta,\sigma^2) =-\frac n2\log(2\pi\sigma^2) -\frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-x_i^\top\beta)^2. $$

Therefore, for fixed $\sigma^2$,

$$\arg\max_\beta\ell(\beta,\sigma^2)=\arg\min_\beta\operatorname{RSS}(\beta).$$

Thus OLS is the maximum-likelihood estimator of $\beta$ under independent Gaussian errors. The optimisation definition is more general; Gaussianity is what gives least squares this particular likelihood interpretation.

3. Deriving the normal equations

Start from

$$\operatorname{RSS}(\beta)=(y-X\beta)^\top(y-X\beta).$$

Expand:

$$ \operatorname{RSS}(\beta) =y^\top y-y^\top X\beta-\beta^\top X^\top y+\beta^\top X^\top X\beta. $$

The two middle terms are equal because each is a scalar, so

$$ \operatorname{RSS}(\beta) =y^\top y-2\beta^\top X^\top y+\beta^\top X^\top X\beta. $$

Using $\nabla_\beta(\beta^\top A\beta)=(A+A^\top)\beta$ and the symmetry of $X^\top X$ gives

$$\nabla_\beta\operatorname{RSS}=-2X^\top y+2X^\top X\beta.$$

At a stationary point,

$$-2X^\top y+2X^\top X\hat\beta=0,$$ hence
$$X^\top X\hat\beta=X^\top y.$$

These are the normal equations. If $X$ has full column rank, then $X^\top X$ is positive definite and invertible, giving

$$\hat\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y.$$

3.1 Why the stationary point is the global minimum

The Hessian is

$$\nabla_\beta^2\operatorname{RSS}=2X^\top X.$$

For any vector $z$,

$$z^\top X^\top Xz=\|Xz\|_2^2\ge0,$$

so $X^\top X$ is always positive semidefinite. If $X$ has full column rank, $Xz=0$ implies $z=0$, hence

$$z^\top X^\top Xz>0\qquad(z\ne0),$$

so the Hessian is positive definite and the objective is strictly convex. Therefore the solution is the unique global minimiser.

3.2 Why they are called "normal" equations

Rearrange the normal equations:

$$X^\top(y-X\hat\beta)=0.$$

Writing $\hat e=y-X\hat\beta$ gives

$$X^\top\hat e=0.$$

Every column of $X$ is orthogonal to the residual vector. The residual is therefore normal to the subspace spanned by the columns of $X$.

4. Projection geometry and the hat matrix

The fitted values are

$$ \hat y=X\hat\beta =X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y. $$

Define

$$H:=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top.$$

Then $\hat y=Hy$. The matrix $H$ is the hat matrix: it puts the hat on $y$.

It is an orthogonal projector onto $\operatorname{col}(X)$. Two properties prove this directly.

Symmetry:

$$ H^\top=\left[X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\right]^\top =X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top=H. $$

Idempotence:

$$ H^2=X(X^\top X)^{-1}\underbrace{X^\top X}_{\text{cancels}}(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top=H. $$

Hence projecting twice changes nothing. The residual vector is

$$\hat e=(I-H)y,$$

and $I-H$ is the complementary projector onto $\operatorname{col}(X)^\perp$.

Because

$$H(I-H)=H-H^2=0,$$

we have $\hat y^\top\hat e=0$. Fitted values and residuals are orthogonal.

4.1 Rank, trace and degrees of freedom

An idempotent matrix has eigenvalues only $0$ or $1$. If $X$ has full column rank $p+1$, then

$$\operatorname{rank}(H)=p+1,$$ and therefore $$\operatorname{tr}(H)=p+1.$$

This is the number of fitted parameters. The residual projector $I-H$ has rank

$$n-(p+1)=n-p-1,$$

which is the residual degrees of freedom.

4.2 The centred ANOVA decomposition

With an intercept in the model, define $y_c=y-\bar y\mathbf 1$ and $\hat y_c=\hat y-\bar y\mathbf 1$. Since $\hat y_c\perp\hat e$,

$$\|y_c\|_2^2=\|\hat y_c\|_2^2+\|\hat e\|_2^2,$$ i.e. $$\mathrm{TSS}=\mathrm{ESS}+\mathrm{RSS}.$$

This gives the familiar

$$R^2=1-\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{\mathrm{TSS}}=\frac{\mathrm{ESS}}{\mathrm{TSS}}.$$

5. OLS as a statistical estimator

Assume the data follow

$$y=X\beta+\varepsilon,\qquad \mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0.$$

Substitute this into the OLS estimator:

$$ \hat\beta =(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top(X\beta+\varepsilon) =\beta+(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\varepsilon. $$

5.1 Unbiasedness

Conditioning on $X$,

$$ \mathbb E[\hat\beta\mid X] =\beta+(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X] =\beta. $$
$$\boxed{\mathbb E[\hat\beta\mid X]=\beta}$$

5.2 Covariance

If, in addition,

$$\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon\mid X)=\sigma^2I,$$ then

$$ \begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta\mid X) &=\operatorname{Var}\!\left((X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\varepsilon\mid X\right)\\ &=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top(\sigma^2I)X(X^\top X)^{-1}\\ &=\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}. \end{aligned} $$
$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta\mid X)=\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}.$$

This formula connects statistical uncertainty directly to the geometry of the design matrix. If $X^\top X$ is nearly singular, some coefficient variances become very large.

6. Gauss–Markov: why OLS is BLUE

Under zero conditional mean, homoscedasticity and uncorrelated errors, OLS is the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator: among estimators linear in $y$ and unbiased for $\beta$, it has the smallest covariance matrix in the positive-semidefinite sense.

Let another linear estimator be

$$\tilde\beta=Cy.$$

Unbiasedness for every $\beta$ requires

$$\mathbb E[Cy\mid X]=CX\beta=\beta,$$ hence $$CX=I.$$

Write

$$C=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top+D.$$

Since both $C$ and $(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top$ are unbiased,

$$DX=0.$$

Now

$$ \operatorname{Var}(\tilde\beta\mid X)=\sigma^2CC^\top. $$ Substituting the decomposition of $C$ gives $$ \begin{aligned} CC^\top &=\left[(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top+D\right] \left[X(X^\top X)^{-1}+D^\top\right]\\ &=(X^\top X)^{-1}+DD^\top, \end{aligned} $$

because the cross terms vanish when $DX=0$. Therefore

$$ \operatorname{Var}(\tilde\beta\mid X)-\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta\mid X) =\sigma^2DD^\top\succeq0. $$

No normality assumption is needed for this result. It compares OLS with linear unbiased estimators; it does not say a biased estimator such as ridge cannot have lower mean-squared error.

7. Sampling distribution and inference

If

$$\varepsilon\mid X\sim\mathcal N(0,\sigma^2I),$$ then $\hat\beta$ is an affine transformation of a Gaussian vector, so

$$\hat\beta\mid X\sim\mathcal N\!\left(\beta,\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\right).$$

The residual sum of squares satisfies

$$\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{\sigma^2}\sim\chi^2_{n-p-1},$$ and is independent of $\hat\beta$. Hence the unbiased estimator

$$s^2=\frac{\mathrm{RSS}}{n-p-1}$$ can replace $\sigma^2$ in coefficient standard errors.

For coefficient $j$,

$$\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_j)=s\sqrt{[(X^\top X)^{-1}]_{jj}},$$ and $$ \frac{\hat\beta_j-\beta_j}{\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_j)} \sim t_{n-p-1}. $$

A two-sided $(1-\alpha)$ confidence interval is therefore

$$ \hat\beta_j\pm t_{1-\alpha/2,n-p-1}\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_j). $$

7.1 Mean-response and prediction intervals

For a new augmented predictor vector $x_*$, the fitted mean is

$$\hat y_*=x_*^\top\hat\beta.$$

Its variance due to coefficient uncertainty is

$$\sigma^2x_*^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}x_*.$$

A new observation also contains fresh noise, so

$$ \operatorname{Var}(y_* - \hat y_*\mid X,x_*) =\sigma^2\left[1+x_*^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}x_*\right]. $$

This is why a prediction interval for a single future observation is wider than a confidence interval for the conditional mean.

8. Numerical linear algebra: why the textbook inverse is not the algorithm

The identity

$$\hat\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$$ is useful for theory. Numerically, explicitly forming the inverse is usually a poor route.

8.1 Conditioning

For full-rank $X$, the 2-norm condition number is

$$\kappa_2(X)=\frac{\sigma_{\max}(X)}{\sigma_{\min}(X)}.$$

The singular values of $X^\top X$ are the squared singular values of $X$, so

$$\kappa_2(X^\top X)=\kappa_2(X)^2.$$

Thus forming the normal-equation matrix squares the conditioning problem. Near-collinear predictors make the smallest singular value small; squaring it makes the numerical separation worse.

8.2 QR factorisation

For full-column-rank $X$, let the thin QR factorisation be

$$X=QR,$$ where $Q^\top Q=I$ and $R$ is upper triangular. Decompose $y$ into a component in $\operatorname{col}(Q)$ and an orthogonal remainder. Then

$$ \|y-X\beta\|_2^2 =\|Q^\top y-R\beta\|_2^2+\|(I-QQ^\top)y\|_2^2. $$

The second term does not depend on $\beta$, so minimising the residual norm reduces to

$$R\hat\beta=Q^\top y.$$

We solve this triangular system by back-substitution. QR avoids constructing $X^\top X$ and therefore avoids squaring its condition number.

8.3 Singular value decomposition

Let

$$X=U\Sigma V^\top.$$

The Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse is

$$X^+=V\Sigma^+U^\top,$$ so the minimum-norm least-squares solution is

$$\hat\beta=X^+y=V\Sigma^+U^\top y.$$

SVD makes numerical rank explicit through the singular values. It remains well defined when $X$ is rank-deficient, where the normal-equation inverse does not exist.

Practical rule. The normal equations are excellent for derivation. Numerical software should solve the least-squares problem through a stable factorisation or an appropriate iterative least-squares method.

9. The optimisation view: gradient descent and Newton's method

It is useful to rescale the objective as

$$J(\beta)=\frac{1}{2n}\|y-X\beta\|_2^2.$$

Then

$$\nabla J(\beta)=\frac1nX^\top(X\beta-y),$$ and $$\nabla^2J(\beta)=\frac1nX^\top X.$$

9.1 Gradient descent

The batch update is

$$\beta_{k+1}=\beta_k-\eta\frac1nX^\top(X\beta_k-y).$$

If $X$ has full column rank, the objective is strongly convex. With a suitable step size, gradient descent converges geometrically. The convergence rate is governed by the spectrum of $X^\top X$; poor conditioning means a long, narrow quadratic bowl and slow progress in the weak-curvature directions.

For this scaling of $J$, a sufficient fixed-step condition is

$$0<\eta<\frac{2n}{\lambda_{\max}(X^\top X)}.$$

9.2 Newton's method lands on OLS in one step

Newton's update is

$$ \beta_{k+1}=\beta_k-[\nabla^2J]^{-1}\nabla J(\beta_k). $$ Substituting the gradient and Hessian, $$ \begin{aligned} \beta_{k+1} &=\beta_k-(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top(X\beta_k-y)\\ &=\beta_k-\beta_k+(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y\\ &=\hat\beta. \end{aligned} $$

Because the objective is exactly quadratic and the Hessian is constant, one Newton step reaches the minimiser from any starting point when $X^\top X$ is invertible.

10. Worked example: one dataset, three views

Take

$$(x,y)=(1,2),(2,2),(3,4).$$

With an intercept,

$$ X=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&2\\1&3\end{pmatrix},\qquad y=\begin{pmatrix}2\\2\\4\end{pmatrix}. $$

10.1 Solve the normal equations

Compute

$$ X^\top X =\begin{pmatrix}3&6\\6&14\end{pmatrix},\qquad X^\top y =\begin{pmatrix}8\\18\end{pmatrix}. $$ Therefore $$ \begin{pmatrix}3&6\\6&14\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}\hat\beta_0\\\hat\beta_1\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}8\\18\end{pmatrix}. $$ The determinant is $42-36=6$, and $$ (X^\top X)^{-1} =\frac16\begin{pmatrix}14&-6\\-6&3\end{pmatrix}. $$ Hence $$ \hat\beta =\frac16 \begin{pmatrix}14&-6\\-6&3\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}8\\18\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}2/3\\1\end{pmatrix}. $$ Thus
$$\hat y=\frac23+x.$$

10.2 Check the residual orthogonality

The fitted values are

$$\hat y=\begin{pmatrix}5/3\\8/3\\11/3\end{pmatrix},$$ so $$ \hat e=y-\hat y =\begin{pmatrix}1/3\\-2/3\\1/3\end{pmatrix}. $$ Now

$$ X^\top\hat e =\begin{pmatrix}1&1&1\\1&2&3\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}1/3\\-2/3\\1/3\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\end{pmatrix}. $$

The first zero says the residuals sum to zero; the second says they are orthogonal to the predictor column. Algebra and geometry are saying the same thing.

11. Near multicollinearity: when coefficients explode

Take two almost identical predictors:

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 80
x1 = rng.normal(size=n)
x2 = x1 + 1e-6 * rng.normal(size=n)
X  = np.column_stack([x1, x2])
y  = 3*x1 + 0.05*rng.normal(size=n)

With an intercept, let $X_d=[\mathbf1,X]$. A least-squares fit gives approximately

$$\hat\beta_1\approx5900.64,\qquad \hat\beta_2\approx-5897.65.$$

The individual coefficients are unstable, yet their sum is about $2.992$, close to the effective slope $3$, and the fitted RMSE is about $0.0458$.

For the augmented design matrix,

$$\kappa_2(X_d)\approx2.12\times10^6,$$ while $$\kappa_2(X_d^\top X_d)\approx4.49\times10^{12}.$$

The covariance formula explains the instability:

$$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta\mid X_d)=\sigma^2(X_d^\top X_d)^{-1}.$$

If an eigenvalue of $X_d^\top X_d$ approaches zero, the corresponding eigenvalue of its inverse becomes large. Small perturbations in the data can then produce large changes in the separate coefficients.

Interpretation. Near multicollinearity is mainly a coefficient-stability and variance problem. Perfect collinearity makes the individual coefficients non-identifiable because the least-squares minimiser is no longer unique.

12. What Python actually does

A transparent implementation should solve the least-squares problem directly rather than explicitly forming $(X^\top X)^{-1}$:

import numpy as np

def fit_ols(X, y, fit_intercept=True):
    X = np.asarray(X, dtype=float)
    y = np.asarray(y, dtype=float)

    if fit_intercept:
        X = np.column_stack([np.ones(len(X)), X])

    beta, residuals, rank, singular_values = np.linalg.lstsq(
        X, y, rcond=None
    )
    return beta, residuals, rank, singular_values

numpy.linalg.lstsq returns a vector minimising $\|y-X\beta\|_2$. When there are several minimisers, it returns the minimum-Euclidean-norm solution.

12.1 scikit-learn

For dense data with the default positive=False, current scikit-learn centres $X$ and $y$ when fit_intercept=True, solves the centred least-squares problem through scipy.linalg.lstsq, and reconstructs the intercept afterwards. Sparse data use an iterative least-squares solver; positive=True changes the problem to non-negative least squares.

from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression

model = LinearRegression()
model.fit(X, y)

print(model.intercept_)
print(model.coef_)
print(model.rank_)
print(model.singular_)

The conceptual distinction is important: OLS defines the estimator; QR, SVD, LAPACK routines and iterative solvers are numerical routes to it.

13. Which assumptions support which claims?

Different statements about linear regression require different assumptions. Keeping them separate avoids much confusion.

ClaimWhat is needed
OLS coefficients minimise RSSNo probabilistic assumption. This follows from the optimisation problem.
The minimiser is uniqueFull column rank of $X$.
$\mathbb E[\hat\beta\mid X]=\beta$Correct linear conditional mean and $\mathbb E[\varepsilon\mid X]=0$.
$\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta\mid X)=\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$Zero conditional mean plus homoscedastic, uncorrelated errors.
Gauss–Markov BLUE resultLinearity, full rank, zero conditional mean and spherical error covariance $\sigma^2I$.
Exact finite-sample $t$ and $F$ distributionsThe classical assumptions plus Gaussian errors.

For prediction, the relevant empirical question is usually out-of-sample error. For coefficient interpretation and uncertainty, the assumptions about the data-generating process and the error structure matter directly.

14. Ridge regression as a natural extension

The instability of OLS under near-collinearity suggests adding curvature in directions where $X^\top X$ is weak. Ridge regression solves

$$ \hat\beta_\lambda =\arg\min_\beta\left\{\|y-X\beta\|_2^2+\lambda\|\beta\|_2^2\right\}. $$ Differentiating gives

$$ -2X^\top y+2X^\top X\beta+2\lambda\beta=0, $$ hence
$$\hat\beta_\lambda=(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y.$$

If $X^\top X=V\Lambda V^\top$, then

$$X^\top X+\lambda I=V(\Lambda+\lambda I)V^\top.$$

Every eigenvalue is shifted upward by $\lambda$. Directions associated with very small eigenvalues are therefore stabilised. Statistically, this reduces variance by accepting some bias.

In models with an intercept, the intercept is normally excluded from the penalty.

15. Under the hood

  • fit() solves a convex least-squares problem: $\min_\beta\|y-X\beta\|_2^2$.
  • Gaussian errors make the same estimator the maximum-likelihood solution.
  • The normal equations arise from setting the gradient to zero; the Hessian explains convexity and uniqueness.
  • $\hat y=Hy$ is an orthogonal projection and $H$ is symmetric and idempotent.
  • $\hat\beta$ is unbiased under zero conditional mean, with covariance $\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$ under spherical errors.
  • Gauss–Markov follows by comparing any linear unbiased estimator with OLS and obtaining a positive-semidefinite covariance difference.
  • The textbook inverse is a theoretical identity. QR and SVD solve the numerical problem more sensibly.
  • Conditioning links numerical instability, multicollinearity and coefficient variance.
  • Gradient descent exposes the optimisation geometry; Newton's method reaches the OLS solution in one step because the objective is quadratic.
  • Ridge modifies the same linear algebra by shifting the eigenvalues of $X^\top X$.

The single call LinearRegression().fit(X, y) therefore hides a substantial chain of applied mathematics: probability, optimisation, geometry, matrix factorisation and statistical inference all meet in one of the simplest models in machine learning.
