Lecture 1 — Linear Regression

Part I — Mathematical Foundations and Classical Supervised Learning
Dr. Muhammad Shoaib · Applied Mathematics & Data Science · LinkedIn newsletter · every lecture follows the same 27 sections — use the sidebar to navigate.

Before we touch a single equation, let me set expectations. Linear regression looks deceptively simple — a line through some points — and for that reason it is the most underestimated algorithm in the entire course. Almost everything that follows is, in some sense, a generalisation of ideas that appear here first: the notion of a loss function, the idea of fitting parameters by optimisation, the geometry of projection, the statistical machinery of estimators and their uncertainty, and the tension between bias and variance. If you understand linear regression completely — not just "call .fit()" but why the normal equations look the way they do, what the Gauss–Markov theorem actually promises, and where the whole edifice collapses — then logistic regression, ridge, neural networks, and Gaussian processes will feel like variations on a theme you already know.

So we will go slowly and build it properly.

1. Motivation and Problem Setting

The problem. We have observations of some quantity $y$ (a response, target, or dependent variable) that we believe depends on one or more measured quantities $x_1, \dots, x_p$ (features, predictors, covariates, independent variables). We want a function $f$ such that $f(x) \approx y$, so that:

  • given a new $x$ we can predict $y$ (the machine-learning goal), and/or
  • we can understand how $y$ changes as each $x_j$ changes (the statistical-inference goal).

Linear regression is the special case where we insist $f$ be an affine function of the features:

$$ f(x) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x_1 + \cdots + \beta_p x_p. $$

Why it was developed. Historically this is the oldest fitted statistical model — Legendre (1805) and Gauss (early 1800s) developed least squares to reconcile noisy astronomical measurements of orbits. The core problem they faced is exactly the modern one: more equations (observations) than unknowns (parameters), all contaminated by error, and no exact solution. Least squares gives the "best compromise."

Where it sits. Linear regression is supervised (we have labelled targets $y$), parametric (a fixed, finite set of parameters $\beta$), and used for regression (continuous $y$). It is the archetypal low-variance, potentially-high-bias model. In the landscape of the course it is the root of a family tree:

  • Replace the identity link with a sigmoid → logistic regression (coming in Week 2).
  • Add an $\ell_2$ penalty → ridge; an $\ell_1$ penalty → lasso.
  • Stack many of these with nonlinear activations → neural networks (coming in Week 23).
  • Put a Gaussian prior on $\beta$ and integrate it out → Bayesian linear regression, whose kernelised limit is a Gaussian process (coming in Week 17).

Assumptions (the classical linear model, in increasing strength):

  1. Linearity: $\mathbb{E}[y \mid x] = \beta_0 + \sum_j \beta_j x_j$. The conditional mean is affine in the parameters.
  2. Full rank / no perfect collinearity: the design matrix $X$ has linearly independent columns (so $X^\top X$ is invertible).
  3. Exogeneity / zero conditional mean of errors: $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid X] = 0$.
  4. Homoscedasticity: $\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i \mid X) = \sigma^2$, constant across observations.
  5. No autocorrelation: $\operatorname{Cov}(\varepsilon_i, \varepsilon_j) = 0$ for $i \neq j$.
  6. (For exact small-sample inference only) Normality: $\varepsilon \mid X \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2 I)$.

When these are reasonable. Assumptions 1–5 are often approximately fine for smooth relationships over a limited range, for engineered features, and when the noise is genuinely additive and roughly constant. Note the word "linear" refers to linearity in the parameters, not in the raw inputs: $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x + \beta_2 x^2$ is still a linear model — it is linear in $\beta$. This is why linear regression is far more flexible than it first appears.

When they are unreasonable. Multiplicative noise (income, prices — variance grows with level) breaks homoscedasticity; time series break independence; curved relationships break linearity; and heavy-tailed outliers wreck the least-squares fit because squared error punishes large residuals enormously.

2. Core Intuition

Suppose you have a scatter of points and you want the "best" line. What does best mean? Least squares says: the line for which the total squared vertical distance from the points to the line is as small as possible.

Three complementary intuitions:

Optimisation intuition. We define a cost that measures misfit — the sum of squared residuals — and we slide the line around (adjust slope and intercept) until the cost bottoms out. Because the cost is a smooth, upward-opening (convex) bowl in the parameters, there is a unique bottom and we can find it exactly by setting derivatives to zero. No local minima, no initialisation worries. This convexity is a gift we will sorely miss in deep learning.

Geometric intuition. Stack the $n$ observed targets into a vector $y \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Every possible fitted-value vector $\hat y = X\beta$ lives in the column space of $X$ — a $(p{+}1)$-dimensional subspace of $\mathbb{R}^n$. We cannot in general reach $y$ exactly (it's outside that subspace, thanks to noise). The closest point in the subspace to $y$ is its orthogonal projection. Least squares is orthogonal projection: $\hat y$ is the shadow of $y$ onto the space spanned by the predictors, and the residual $y - \hat y$ is perpendicular to that space. Hold this picture; it explains almost everything.

Probabilistic intuition. Assume $y_i = x_i^\top \beta + \varepsilon_i$ with $\varepsilon_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \sigma^2)$ independent. Then fitting by maximum likelihood — finding the $\beta$ that makes the observed data most probable — turns out to be identical to least squares. The Gaussian's $e^{-\text{(residual)}^2/2\sigma^2}$ form means maximising log-likelihood minimises summed squared residuals. So "minimise squared error" is not arbitrary; it is the maximum-likelihood answer under Gaussian noise.

Tiny numerical seed. Points $(1,2),(2,2),(3,4)$. Eyeballing, the line rises by about 1 per unit $x$ and passes near $y\approx1$ at $x=0$. We'll compute the exact least-squares line by hand in §6 and get slope $1$, intercept $\tfrac{2}{3}$.

3. Mathematical Formulation

Notation. We have $n$ observations. For observation $i$, let $x_i \in \mathbb{R}^{p}$ be its feature vector and $y_i \in \mathbb{R}$ its target. To absorb the intercept, we prepend a constant $1$, so the augmented feature vector is $\tilde x_i = (1, x_{i1}, \dots, x_{ip})^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{p+1}$. Collect everything:

$$ X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & x_{11} & \cdots & x_{1p} \\ 1 & x_{21} & \cdots & x_{2p} \\ \vdots & & & \vdots \\ 1 & x_{n1} & \cdots & x_{np} \end{pmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times (p+1)}, \qquad y = \begin{pmatrix} y_1 \\ \vdots \\ y_n \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \beta = \begin{pmatrix} \beta_0 \\ \vdots \\ \beta_p \end{pmatrix}. $$

$X$ is the design matrix. Each row is an observation; each column is a feature (the first column is all ones).

The model.

$$ y = X\beta + \varepsilon, \qquad \mathbb{E}[\varepsilon \mid X] = 0, \quad \operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon \mid X) = \sigma^2 I_n. $$

The loss (objective). The residual sum of squares:

$$ \text{RSS}(\beta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \big(y_i - x_i^\top \beta\big)^2 = \|y - X\beta\|_2^2 = (y - X\beta)^\top (y - X\beta). $$

We seek $\hat\beta = \arg\min_\beta \text{RSS}(\beta)$.

Probabilistic form (likelihood). Under Gaussian noise, each observation has density

$$ p(y_i \mid x_i, \beta, \sigma^2) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(y_i - x_i^\top\beta)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right). $$

By independence the likelihood is the product, and the log-likelihood is

$$ \ell(\beta, \sigma^2) = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\sum_{i=1}^n (y_i - x_i^\top\beta)^2 = -\frac{n}{2}\log(2\pi\sigma^2) - \frac{\text{RSS}(\beta)}{2\sigma^2}. $$

Because only the last term depends on $\beta$, and it is $-\text{RSS}/(2\sigma^2)$, maximising $\ell$ over $\beta$ is exactly minimising RSS. Least squares $\equiv$ Gaussian MLE.

Regularised variants (introduced now, expanded in §19):

$$ \text{Ridge: } \min_\beta \|y - X\beta\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\beta_{1:p}\|_2^2, \qquad \text{Lasso: } \min_\beta \|y - X\beta\|_2^2 + \lambda \|\beta_{1:p}\|_1. $$

(The intercept $\beta_0$ is conventionally left unpenalised.)

4. Derivation

4.1 The normal equations and the OLS estimator

Expand the objective:

$$ \text{RSS}(\beta) = (y - X\beta)^\top(y - X\beta) = y^\top y - 2\beta^\top X^\top y + \beta^\top X^\top X \beta. $$

(The middle term: $y^\top X\beta$ and $\beta^\top X^\top y$ are scalars equal to each other, hence the factor 2.)

Take the gradient with respect to $\beta$. Using $\nabla_\beta (\beta^\top A \beta) = 2A\beta$ for symmetric $A$, and $\nabla_\beta(b^\top \beta) = b$:

$$ \nabla_\beta \text{RSS} = -2 X^\top y + 2 X^\top X \beta. $$

Set to zero:

$$ X^\top X \,\hat\beta = X^\top y \qquad \text{(the normal equations).} $$

If $X^\top X$ is invertible (full column rank),

$$ \hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top y. $$

To confirm this is a minimum, the Hessian is $\nabla^2_\beta \text{RSS} = 2X^\top X$, which is positive semidefinite always, and positive definite when $X$ has full column rank. A convex function with a positive-definite Hessian has a unique global minimum.

Why "normal" equations? Rewrite them as $X^\top(y - X\hat\beta) = 0$, i.e. $X^\top \hat\varepsilon = 0$: every column of $X$ is orthogonal (normal) to the residual vector. This is the algebraic statement of the projection picture.

4.2 The projection / hat matrix

The fitted values are

$$ \hat y = X\hat\beta = X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y = H y, \qquad H := X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top. $$

$H$ is the hat matrix ("puts the hat on $y$"). It is the orthogonal projector onto the column space of $X$:

  • Symmetric: $H^\top = H$.
  • Idempotent: $H^2 = H$ (projecting twice = projecting once).
  • Its eigenvalues are all $0$ or $1$; $\operatorname{trace}(H) = p+1$ = dimension of the column space = number of parameters.

The residuals are $\hat\varepsilon = (I - H)y$, and $I - H$ projects onto the orthogonal complement. Since $H(I-H) = 0$, fitted values and residuals are orthogonal — Pythagoras gives the ANOVA decomposition $\|y\|^2 = \|\hat y\|^2 + \|\hat\varepsilon\|^2$ (after centering).

4.3 Gauss–Markov theorem (why OLS is special)

Claim. Under assumptions 1–5 (linearity, full rank, zero-mean errors, homoscedasticity, no autocorrelation — no normality needed), the OLS estimator $\hat\beta$ is BLUE: the Best Linear Unbiased Estimator — the one with smallest variance among all estimators that are (a) linear in $y$ and (b) unbiased.

Unbiasedness. $\hat\beta = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top(X\beta + \varepsilon) = \beta + (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top \varepsilon$. Taking expectations, since $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid X]=0$: $\mathbb{E}[\hat\beta \mid X] = \beta$.

Variance.

$$ \operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta \mid X) = (X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top \,\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon)\, X (X^\top X)^{-1} = \sigma^2 (X^\top X)^{-1} $$

using $\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon) = \sigma^2 I$.

Optimality sketch. Any other linear unbiased estimator can be written $\tilde\beta = ((X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top + D)y$ for some matrix $D$. Unbiasedness forces $DX = 0$. Working out $\operatorname{Var}(\tilde\beta) = \sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1} + \sigma^2 DD^\top$, and since $DD^\top$ is positive semidefinite, $\operatorname{Var}(\tilde\beta) \succeq \operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta)$. OLS wins.

The theorem's punchline: if the classical assumptions hold, you cannot beat OLS with any other linear unbiased estimator. The escape hatch — and it's the whole justification for ridge/lasso — is to allow biased estimators, which can have dramatically lower variance and thus lower total error.

4.4 Estimating $\sigma^2$

An unbiased estimator of the noise variance is

$$ \hat\sigma^2 = \frac{\text{RSS}}{n - (p+1)} = \frac{\|\hat\varepsilon\|^2}{n - p - 1}. $$

The denominator uses degrees of freedom $n - p - 1$, not $n$: we "spent" $p+1$ degrees fitting the parameters, and the residuals are constrained to lie in an $(n - p - 1)$-dimensional space (the orthogonal complement of the column space). Dividing by $n$ would underestimate $\sigma^2$.

5. Algorithm

Closed form (small/medium $p$).

Input: design matrix X (n × (p+1), first column ones), targets y (n)
1. Form the Gram matrix    G = Xᵀ X          # (p+1)×(p+1)
2. Form the moment vector  c = Xᵀ y          # (p+1)
3. Solve the linear system  G β = c  for β    # DO NOT invert G explicitly
4. Fitted values:  ŷ = X β
5. Residuals:      e = y − ŷ
6. σ̂² = (eᵀe) / (n − p − 1)
7. Cov(β̂) = σ̂² G⁻¹   → standard errors are sqrt of diagonal
Output: β̂, σ̂², standard errors, fitted values, residuals

Numerically stable practice: never compute $(X^\top X)^{-1}$ explicitly. Instead solve via the QR decomposition $X = QR$ (then $\hat\beta = R^{-1}Q^\top y$, a triangular solve) or the SVD $X = U\Sigma V^\top$ (then $\hat\beta = V\Sigma^{-1}U^\top y$). Forming $X^\top X$ squares the condition number, amplifying rounding error; QR/SVD work on $X$ directly.

Iterative (huge $n$ or $p$). When $X$ doesn't fit in memory, minimise RSS by gradient descent (§9): $\beta \leftarrow \beta - \eta X^\top(X\beta - y)$, or stochastic gradient descent on mini-batches.

6. Worked Numerical Example

Data: $(x,y) = (1,2), (2,2), (3,4)$. Fit $y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 x$.

$$ X = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 2 \\ 1 & 3 \end{pmatrix}, \quad y = \begin{pmatrix} 2 \\ 2 \\ 4 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Step 1 — Gram matrix $X^\top X$:

$$ X^\top X = \begin{pmatrix} 3 & 6 \\ 6 & 14 \end{pmatrix} $$

(top-left = number of points = 3; top-right = $\sum x_i = 6$; bottom-right = $\sum x_i^2 = 1+4+9 = 14$).

Step 2 — moment vector $X^\top y$:

$$ X^\top y = \begin{pmatrix} \sum y_i \\ \sum x_i y_i \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ 2\cdot1 + 2\cdot2 + 4\cdot3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ 18 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Step 3 — invert the $2\times2$ Gram matrix. Determinant $= 3\cdot14 - 6\cdot6 = 42 - 36 = 6$.

$$ (X^\top X)^{-1} = \frac{1}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 14 & -6 \\ -6 & 3 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Step 4 — solve for $\hat\beta$:

$$ \hat\beta = \frac{1}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 14 & -6 \\ -6 & 3 \end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} 8 \\ 18 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 112 - 108 \\ -48 + 54 \end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{6}\begin{pmatrix} 4 \\ 6 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 2/3 \\ 1 \end{pmatrix}. $$

So $\hat\beta_0 = \tfrac{2}{3} \approx 0.667$, $\hat\beta_1 = 1$. The line is $\hat y = \tfrac{2}{3} + x$.

Step 5 — fitted values and residuals:

$$ \hat y = \begin{pmatrix} 2/3 + 1 \\ 2/3 + 2 \\ 2/3 + 3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 5/3 \\ 8/3 \\ 11/3 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \hat\varepsilon = y - \hat y = \begin{pmatrix} 2 - 5/3 \\ 2 - 8/3 \\ 4 - 11/3 \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 1/3 \\ -2/3 \\ 1/3 \end{pmatrix}. $$

Sanity checks. Residuals sum to $\tfrac13 - \tfrac23 + \tfrac13 = 0$ (guaranteed by the intercept column: $X^\top\hat\varepsilon = 0$ includes the all-ones row). And $\sum x_i \hat\varepsilon_i = 1\cdot\tfrac13 + 2\cdot(-\tfrac23) + 3\cdot\tfrac13 = \tfrac13 - \tfrac43 + 1 = 0$ ✓ — residuals orthogonal to the $x$ column, exactly the normal equations.

RSS $= (\tfrac13)^2 + (\tfrac23)^2 + (\tfrac13)^2 = \tfrac19 + \tfrac49 + \tfrac19 = \tfrac69 = \tfrac23$.
$\hat\sigma^2 = \text{RSS}/(n - p - 1) = (2/3)/(3 - 2) = \tfrac23$.

$R^2$: total sum of squares about $\bar y = 8/3$: $\text{TSS} = (2-\tfrac83)^2 + (2-\tfrac83)^2 + (4-\tfrac83)^2 = \tfrac49 + \tfrac49 + \tfrac{16}{9} = \tfrac{24}{9} = \tfrac83$. Then $R^2 = 1 - \text{RSS}/\text{TSS} = 1 - \tfrac{2/3}{8/3} = 1 - \tfrac14 = 0.75$.

7. Geometry and Visual Interpretation

There are two geometric pictures, and confusing them is a common source of muddle.

Feature-space picture ($\mathbb{R}^{p+1}$, "the scatterplot"). Points live in feature-by-target space; the model is a hyperplane (a line when $p=1$). Fitting tilts and shifts the hyperplane to minimise summed squared vertical distances. This is the picture most people draw. A good visualisation: scatter of $(x,y)$, the fitted line, and short vertical stubs marking each residual.

Observation-space picture ($\mathbb{R}^n$, "the projection"). This is the deeper one. The target vector $y$ is a single point in $n$-dimensional space. The columns of $X$ span a $(p+1)$-dimensional subspace (the column space). $\hat y$ is the orthogonal projection of $y$ onto that subspace — the closest reachable point. The residual $\hat\varepsilon = y - \hat y$ is the perpendicular dropped from $y$ to the subspace. A good visualisation: a flat plane (the column space) sitting in 3D, the point $y$ floating above it, and $\hat y$ as its foot-of-perpendicular, with the right-angle mark between $\hat\varepsilon$ and the plane. This picture is the normal equations and is the Gauss–Markov geometry; the Pythagorean relation $\|y\|^2 = \|\hat y\|^2 + \|\hat\varepsilon\|^2$ falls straight out.

$R^2$ geometrically is $\cos^2\theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between the centered $y$ and its projection onto the centered column space. Perfect fit → $y$ lies in the subspace → $\theta = 0$ → $R^2 = 1$.

8. Statistical Interpretation

Linear regression is where machine learning and classical inference genuinely overlap, so it repays careful reading.

$\hat\beta$ as an estimator. It is a random quantity (depends on the random $\varepsilon$). We showed it is unbiased ($\mathbb{E}[\hat\beta]=\beta$) and has covariance $\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$. It is also consistent ($\hat\beta \to \beta$ as $n\to\infty$, provided $\tfrac1n X^\top X$ converges to an invertible matrix) and, under normal errors, efficient (attains the Cramér–Rao lower bound — no unbiased estimator does better).

Standard errors. $\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_j) = \hat\sigma \sqrt{[(X^\top X)^{-1}]_{jj}}$. Small when: noise is small, sample is large, the feature has high variance, and it's uncorrelated with other features (that last is why multicollinearity inflates SEs — see §18).

Hypothesis testing. Under normality, $\dfrac{\hat\beta_j - \beta_j}{\operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_j)} \sim t_{\,n-p-1}$. The usual "is this coefficient significant?" $t$-test compares $\hat\beta_j / \operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_j)$ to a $t$ distribution; the $F$-test compares nested models.

Confidence interval for a coefficient:

$$ \hat\beta_j \pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\,n-p-1}\cdot \operatorname{SE}(\hat\beta_j). $$

This is an interval for a parameter — a statement about our uncertainty in the slope.

Confidence interval for the mean response at a new point $x_\ast$: uncertainty in $\mathbb{E}[y\mid x_\ast] = x_\ast^\top\beta$, with variance $\sigma^2 x_\ast^\top (X^\top X)^{-1} x_\ast$.

Prediction interval for a new observation $y_\ast$: this must additionally include the irreducible noise $\sigma^2$, so its variance is $\sigma^2\big(1 + x_\ast^\top (X^\top X)^{-1} x_\ast\big)$ — always wider than the confidence interval. This distinction — where will the average land vs where will a single new point land — is one of the most frequently botched ideas in applied work.

ML vs inference framing. A machine learner asks "does the model predict new $y$ well?" and reaches for cross-validated test error. A statistician asks "is $\beta_3$ different from zero, and how sure am I?" and reaches for $t$-tests and CIs. Same fitted object, different questions. Crucially, the inference machinery (SEs, $p$-values) is only valid if the assumptions hold and you didn't peek at the data to choose the model — the ML habit of trying many feature sets silently invalidates the classical $p$-values.

9. Training and Optimisation

Closed form. Because RSS is convex quadratic, the minimiser is the exact solution of the normal equations — no iteration needed. Solve $X^\top X\,\hat\beta = X^\top y$ by Cholesky (fast, if well-conditioned), or fit via QR/SVD directly on $X$ (more stable). Cost is dominated by the factorisation (see §12).

When to iterate instead. For very large $n$ (rows don't fit in memory) or very large $p$, or online/streaming settings, use gradient methods. The gradient is $\nabla \text{RSS} = 2X^\top(X\beta - y)$.

  • Batch gradient descent: $\beta \leftarrow \beta - \eta\, X^\top(X\beta - y)$. Converges linearly; the rate depends on the condition number $\kappa(X^\top X)$ — ill-conditioned (correlated, unscaled) features converge painfully slowly, which is a practical reason to standardise features before gradient-based fitting.
  • Stochastic gradient descent (SGD): update using one sample (or mini-batch) at a time. Cheap per step, scales to enormous data, noisy convergence. This is the exact same optimiser that trains neural networks — linear regression is the simplest place to meet it.
  • Newton's method: since the Hessian $2X^\top X$ is constant, one Newton step lands exactly on the solution — Newton's method on a quadratic is the normal equations. This is a clean illustration of why Newton is "second-order": it uses curvature to jump straight to the bottom of a bowl.

Convergence. The objective is strongly convex whenever $X^\top X \succ 0$, guaranteeing a unique optimum and geometric convergence of gradient descent with a suitable step size $\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}(X^\top X)$.

10. Hyperparameters

Plain OLS has no hyperparameters — the estimator is determined once you fix the features. The knobs appear when you regularise or engineer features.

$\lambda$ (ridge/lasso regularisation strength).

  • Mathematically: the weight on the penalty $\lambda\|\beta\|^2$ (or $\|\beta\|_1$). Ridge shrinks the solution toward zero: $\hat\beta_{\text{ridge}} = (X^\top X + \lambda I)^{-1}X^\top y$.
  • Increasing $\lambda$: stronger shrinkage → smaller coefficients → more bias, less variance; at $\lambda\to\infty$ all coefficients (except intercept) vanish.
  • Decreasing $\lambda$: approaches OLS → less bias, more variance; at $\lambda = 0$ you recover OLS.
  • Computational cost: negligible change (still one linear solve per $\lambda$); the cost is in the cross-validation search over $\lambda$.
  • Guidance: choose by cross-validation over a log-spaced grid. Always standardise features first, otherwise the penalty treats a feature measured in millimetres differently from one in kilometres.

Choice of features / polynomial degree $d$. Effectively the model-capacity dial. Higher degree → lower bias, higher variance, and worse conditioning (powers of $x$ become collinear). Treat as a hyperparameter selected by CV.

$\ell_1$ vs $\ell_2$ (elastic-net mixing $\alpha$). Controls the type of regularisation: $\ell_1$ produces sparse solutions (feature selection), $\ell_2$ shrinks smoothly. Elastic net interpolates.

Learning rate $\eta$, batch size (only if using SGD). Standard SGD knobs; irrelevant if you use the closed form.

11. Bias–Variance Behaviour

Decompose expected squared prediction error at a point:

$$ \mathbb{E}\big[(y_0 - \hat f(x_0))^2\big] = \underbrace{\big(\mathbb{E}[\hat f(x_0)] - f(x_0)\big)^2}_{\text{bias}^2} + \underbrace{\operatorname{Var}(\hat f(x_0))}_{\text{variance}} + \underbrace{\sigma^2}_{\text{irreducible}}. $$

OLS is a low-variance/high-bias-prone member of this trade-off — but which way it leans depends on features:

  • Underfitting (high bias): the true relationship is curved but you fit a straight line, or you omit an important feature. The model cannot represent the truth; error persists no matter how much data you collect. Symptom: high error on both train and test.
  • Overfitting (high variance): too many features relative to $n$ (large $p$, small $n$), or high-degree polynomials. The variance term $\sigma^2\,\text{trace}((X^\top X)^{-1})$ blows up — coefficients swing wildly with tiny data perturbations. Symptom: near-zero train error, large test error. In the extreme $p \geq n$, $X^\top X$ is singular and OLS is undefined.
  • Regularisation deliberately injects bias (shrinks $\hat\beta$) to buy a larger reduction in variance — often lowering total error. This is the single most important idea in the whole bias–variance story and recurs in every model in this course.
  • Data size: OLS variance scales like $\sigma^2/n$ per coefficient. More data shrinks variance but never touches bias — a misspecified linear model stays biased at $n = \infty$.

12. Computational Complexity

Let $n$ = observations, $p$ = features.

Training (closed form):

  • Form $X^\top X$: $O(np^2)$. Factorise/invert the $(p{+}1)\times(p{+}1)$ system: $O(p^3)$. Total $O(np^2 + p^3)$.
  • QR on $X$ directly: $O(np^2)$ (preferred; avoids the $p^3$ term dominating and is more stable).
  • When $n \gg p$ (typical), cost is $O(np^2)$ — linear in $n$, which is excellent.

Training (iterative): each gradient step is $O(np)$; total $O(np \cdot T)$ for $T$ iterations. SGD step is $O(p)$.

Prediction: $O(p)$ per new point — a single dot product. Extremely cheap; this is a major deployment advantage.

Memory: $O(np)$ to hold $X$, or just $O(p^2)$ if you stream and accumulate $X^\top X$ and $X^\top y$ incrementally (a nice trick for out-of-core fitting).

Scalability consequences: linear regression is one of the most scalable models. The $p^3$ term only bites when $p$ is in the thousands; the $np^2$ term dominates for wide-but-not-huge data. For truly massive $p$, switch to iterative solvers or dimensionality reduction.

13. Data Preparation

Required / strongly advised:

  • Encoding categoricals: one-hot encode nominal categories (drop one level to avoid perfect collinearity with the intercept — the "dummy variable trap").
  • Handling nonlinearity: if a relationship is curved, add polynomial or spline features, or transform (e.g. log). The model is only as flexible as the features you give it.
  • Outlier scrutiny: squared loss makes OLS acutely sensitive to outliers — one wild point can dominate the fit. Inspect, and consider robust alternatives (Huber loss, RANSAC) if outliers are genuine.
  • Multicollinearity check: examine correlations / variance inflation factors (VIF). Highly correlated predictors destabilise coefficients.
  • Missing values: OLS needs complete cases; impute thoughtfully or model missingness.
  • Skewed targets/features: log-transforming a right-skewed positive target often restores homoscedasticity and linearity simultaneously.

Scaling / standardisation: Not required for plain OLS correctness — the closed-form solution is invariant to linear rescaling of features (coefficients simply rescale to compensate). But it is required for (a) ridge/lasso, where the penalty is scale-dependent, (b) gradient-descent fitting, where it fixes conditioning and speeds convergence, and (c) comparing coefficient magnitudes as "importances." Contrast this with SVM/KNN (coming in Week 3, 5) where scaling changes the answer, not just the numerics.

Time ordering / stationarity: only relevant if data are temporal — then independence of errors is suspect and you'd move toward time-series methods (coming in Week 18).

14. Evaluation

Regression metrics:

  • MAE $= \tfrac1n\sum|y_i - \hat y_i|$ — mean absolute error; robust to outliers, in the units of $y$, but not differentiable at 0.
  • MSE $= \tfrac1n\sum(y_i - \hat y_i)^2$ — penalises large errors quadratically; the quantity OLS actually minimises; units are $y^2$.
  • RMSE $= \sqrt{\text{MSE}}$ — back in the units of $y$; the default reporting metric.
  • $R^2 = 1 - \text{RSS}/\text{TSS}$ — fraction of variance explained. Caveat: $R^2$ never decreases when you add features, even useless ones, so it cannot be used to compare models of different sizes.
  • Adjusted $R^2 = 1 - \dfrac{\text{RSS}/(n-p-1)}{\text{TSS}/(n-1)}$ — penalises extra parameters, so it can go down if a feature doesn't earn its keep. Use this for model comparison.

Why metrics mislead:

  • A high $R^2$ can coexist with a badly misspecified model (curved residuals) — it measures variance explained, not correctness of form.
  • RMSE is dominated by a few large errors; if you care about typical performance, report MAE too.
  • $R^2$ is meaningless out of sample if computed on the training set — always report on held-out data.
  • On non-stationary or extrapolated data, in-sample fit says nothing about future error.

15. Validation Strategy

  • Train/test split for a quick honest estimate; fine when $n$ is large.
  • $k$-fold cross-validation (typically $k=5$ or $10$) for reliable estimates and for tuning $\lambda$. Report mean ± SD across folds.
  • Nested CV when you both tune hyperparameters and estimate generalisation — the outer loop estimates error, the inner loop tunes. Skipping nesting optimistically biases your error estimate.
  • Grouped CV when observations cluster (e.g. multiple rows per patient) — keep whole groups together to avoid leakage.
  • Time-series / rolling-origin validation if data are temporal — never let future leak into the training of past predictions.

Leakage risks specific to regression: standardising or imputing using statistics computed on the whole dataset before splitting leaks test information; fit all preprocessing inside the CV fold (use a pipeline). Feature selection done on the full data before CV is the classic silent leak that inflates reported performance.

16. Diagnostics

The residuals are your microscope. For a well-specified model they should look like structureless noise.

  • Residuals vs fitted: should be a flat, patternless band around zero. A curve → missing nonlinearity (violated linearity). A funnel (spreading) → heteroscedasticity.
  • Q–Q plot of residuals: points on the diagonal → approximately normal errors (needed for valid small-sample inference). Heavy tails → outliers / non-normality.
  • Scale–location plot: checks constant variance more directly.
  • Leverage and Cook's distance: identify high-leverage points (extreme $x$) and influential points that individually swing the fit. High leverage = large diagonal entry $h_{ii}$ of the hat matrix.
  • Residuals vs each predictor: localises where nonlinearity lives.
  • Autocorrelation (Durbin–Watson, ACF of residuals): for ordered data, checks the independence assumption.
  • VIF: flags multicollinearity ($\text{VIF}_j = 1/(1 - R_j^2)$ where $R_j^2$ regresses feature $j$ on the others; VIF > 5–10 is a warning).
  • Learning curves: train vs validation error against $n$ — converging high errors signal bias (underfit); a persistent gap signals variance (overfit).
  • Partial dependence / coefficient plots with CIs: communicate effect sizes and their uncertainty.

17. Strengths

Each advantage traced to its mechanism:

  • Closed-form, globally optimal solution. Convex quadratic loss → unique minimum found exactly by one linear solve. No initialisation, no local minima, fully reproducible.
  • Interpretability. Each $\hat\beta_j$ is the estimated change in $\mathbb{E}[y]$ per unit change in $x_j$, holding others fixed — a directly meaningful quantity, unlike the opaque weights of most models.
  • Rigorous uncertainty quantification. Because the estimator's sampling distribution is known in closed form ($\hat\beta \sim \mathcal{N}(\beta, \sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1})$ under normality), we get exact standard errors, CIs, prediction intervals, and hypothesis tests — few ML models offer this.
  • Optimality guarantee. Gauss–Markov: BLUE under mild assumptions; no linear unbiased estimator does better.
  • Cheap and scalable. $O(np^2)$ training, $O(p)$ prediction; streams to out-of-core with constant memory.
  • Statistical efficiency in low-data regimes. With few parameters, it needs far less data than flexible models to estimate its coefficients precisely — it's the sensible default when $n$ is small.
  • A transparent baseline. If a complex model can't beat well-featured linear regression, that's diagnostic information about your problem.

18. Weaknesses and Failure Modes

  • Bias under misspecification. If $\mathbb{E}[y\mid x]$ is nonlinear and you don't engineer features to capture it, the model is systematically wrong and no amount of data fixes it. Example: fitting a line to a clearly quadratic $y = x^2$ relationship gives residuals that curve like a smile — a dead giveaway.
  • Outlier fragility. Squared loss weights a residual of 10 as 100× a residual of 1. Example: one mistyped data point (a salary of £5,000,000 instead of £50,000) can visibly tilt the entire fitted line.
  • Multicollinearity. When predictors are highly correlated, $X^\top X$ is near-singular; $(X^\top X)^{-1}$ has huge entries, so coefficient variances explode. Example: including both "height in cm" and "height in inches" — coefficients become enormous, opposite-signed, and unstable, though predictions may still be fine. Regularisation or dropping a feature fixes it.
  • Extrapolation danger. The linear form is imposed globally; predicting far outside the training range assumes the line continues, which it usually doesn't.
  • Heteroscedasticity breaks inference. If error variance grows with $\hat y$, OLS point estimates stay unbiased but standard errors are wrong → invalid $p$-values and CIs. Fix with weighted least squares or robust (sandwich) standard errors.
  • $p \geq n$ collapse. More features than observations makes $X^\top X$ singular — OLS is undefined. You must regularise (ridge always works because $X^\top X + \lambda I$ is invertible) or reduce dimensionality.
  • Correlated errors. Time series / clustered data violate independence; naive OLS understates uncertainty.
  • Misleading $R^2$. A high $R^2$ on training data with structured residuals falsely signals a good model.

19. Comparison With Related Algorithms

Linear Regression vs Ridge vs Lasso. All minimise squared error; they differ in the penalty:

Geometrically, ridge's circular constraint region shrinks all coefficients smoothly; lasso's diamond-shaped region has corners on the axes, so the optimum tends to land on an axis (a zero coefficient) — that's why $\ell_1$ produces sparsity.

Linear vs polynomial/spline regression. Same machinery, richer features; trades bias for variance and interpretability.

Linear regression → Logistic regression (coming in Week 2). Same linear predictor $x^\top\beta$, but wrapped in a sigmoid and fitted by maximising a Bernoulli (not Gaussian) likelihood. Linear regression is to continuous targets what logistic is to binary — both are generalised linear models.

Linear regression vs tree ensembles / neural nets. Trees and nets capture nonlinearities and interactions automatically (high capacity, low interpretability, need more data); linear regression needs you to hand-craft those, but rewards you with interpretability, uncertainty, and data efficiency. On genuinely linear, low-dimensional, small-$n$ problems, linear regression often wins.

20. When I Should Use It

Use linear regression when:

  • The relationship is plausibly (approximately) linear, or you can engineer features to make it so.
  • You need interpretable coefficients or statistical inference (effect sizes, significance, CIs).
  • Data are limited — few parameters estimate reliably from little data.
  • You need a fast, cheap, reproducible baseline before reaching for anything complex.
  • You need calibrated uncertainty (prediction intervals) with minimal assumptions machinery.
  • $n \gg p$ and predictors aren't badly collinear.

Add ridge/lasso when: features are many and correlated, $p$ approaches or exceeds $n$, or you want automatic feature selection (lasso).

Avoid (or heavily augment) linear regression when:

  • The relationship is strongly nonlinear with unknown form and you can't feature-engineer it → use trees, GBMs, or neural nets.
  • Important interactions exist that you can't specify by hand.
  • The target is bounded/categorical/count — use the appropriate GLM (logistic, Poisson).
  • Data are riddled with outliers → robust regression.
  • You care only about raw predictive accuracy on large, complex tabular data → gradient boosting usually beats it.

21. Python Implementation From Scratch

import numpy as np

class LinearRegressionScratch:
    """OLS via the normal equations, solved stably (no explicit inverse)."""

    def __init__(self, fit_intercept=True):
        self.fit_intercept = fit_intercept
        self.beta_ = None          # fitted coefficients (incl. intercept if used)
        self.sigma2_ = None        # unbiased noise-variance estimate
        self.cov_beta_ = None      # covariance matrix of the estimator

    def _design(self, X):
        # Prepend a column of ones so beta[0] becomes the intercept.
        if self.fit_intercept:
            return np.hstack([np.ones((X.shape[0], 1)), X])
        return X

    def fit(self, X, y):
        Xd = self._design(np.asarray(X, float))
        y  = np.asarray(y, float).ravel()

        # Normal equations: (Xᵀ X) beta = Xᵀ y.
        # np.linalg.lstsq solves this in a least-squares, numerically stable way
        # (uses SVD internally), and works even if XᵀX is near-singular.
        self.beta_, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(Xd, y, rcond=None)

        # Residuals and unbiased variance estimate: RSS / (n - #params).
        resid = y - Xd @ self.beta_
        n, k = Xd.shape
        self.sigma2_ = resid @ resid / (n - k)

        # Cov(beta) = sigma^2 (XᵀX)^{-1}; gives standard errors on the diagonal.
        XtX_inv = np.linalg.inv(Xd.T @ Xd)
        self.cov_beta_ = self.sigma2_ * XtX_inv
        return self

    def predict(self, X):
        return self._design(np.asarray(X, float)) @ self.beta_

    def standard_errors(self):
        return np.sqrt(np.diag(self.cov_beta_))

# --- demo on the hand-worked example -------------------------------
X = np.array([[1], [2], [3]], float)
y = np.array([2, 2, 4], float)

model = LinearRegressionScratch().fit(X, y)
print("beta  :", model.beta_)          # ~ [0.6667, 1.0]  (intercept, slope)
print("sigma2:", model.sigma2_)        # ~ 0.6667
print("SEs   :", model.standard_errors())
print("pred @ x=4:", model.predict([[4]]))  # 0.6667 + 4 = 4.6667

Line-by-line correspondence to the maths.

  • _design builds the augmented matrix $X$ from §3 — the ones-column is the intercept term $\beta_0$.
  • np.linalg.lstsq(Xd, y) solves the normal equations $X^\top X\hat\beta = X^\top y$ (§4.1) via SVD, the stable route from §5 (better than forming and inverting $X^\top X$).
  • resid @ resid / (n - k) is $\hat\sigma^2 = \text{RSS}/(n-p-1)$ (§4.4) — note the degrees-of-freedom denominator.
  • self.sigma2_ * XtX_inv is $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta) = \sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$ (§4.3); its diagonal square-roots are the standard errors of §8.

(Optional gradient-descent version, to expose the optimiser used later in the course:)

def fit_gd(X, y, eta=0.01, iters=5000):
    Xd = np.hstack([np.ones((len(X), 1)), X])
    beta = np.zeros(Xd.shape[1])
    n = len(y)
    for _ in range(iters):
        grad = (2/n) * Xd.T @ (Xd @ beta - y)   # ∇RSS = 2Xᵀ(Xβ − y)
        beta -= eta * grad                       # descend the convex bowl
    return beta

import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
from sklearn.compose import ColumnTransformer
from sklearn.pipeline import Pipeline
from sklearn.preprocessing import StandardScaler, OneHotEncoder
from sklearn.linear_model import LinearRegression, RidgeCV
from sklearn.model_selection import cross_val_score, KFold
from sklearn.metrics import mean_squared_error, r2_score
import statsmodels.api as sm

# ---- 1. sklearn: prediction-focused, with a leak-free pipeline ----
# Suppose df has numeric + categorical predictors and a target column.
num_features = ["area", "rooms", "age"]
cat_features = ["neighbourhood"]

preprocess = ColumnTransformer([
    ("num", StandardScaler(), num_features),                 # scale numerics
    ("cat", OneHotEncoder(drop="first", handle_unknown="ignore"), cat_features),
])

# RidgeCV picks lambda (alpha) by internal cross-validation — robust default.
model = Pipeline([
    ("prep", preprocess),
    ("reg",  RidgeCV(alphas=np.logspace(-3, 3, 25))),
])

# Honest generalisation estimate: preprocessing is refit inside each fold,
# so no test statistics leak into training.
cv = KFold(n_splits=5, shuffle=True, random_state=0)
neg_mse = cross_val_score(model, df[num_features + cat_features], df["price"],
                          scoring="neg_root_mean_squared_error", cv=cv)
print("CV RMSE:", -neg_mse.mean(), "±", neg_mse.std())

model.fit(df[num_features + cat_features], df["price"])
print("chosen alpha (lambda):", model.named_steps["reg"].alpha_)

# ---- 2. statsmodels: inference-focused (SEs, p-values, CIs, diagnostics) ----
X_sm = sm.add_constant(df[num_features])       # explicit intercept column
ols  = sm.OLS(df["price"], X_sm).fit()
print(ols.summary())                           # coeffs, SEs, t, p, R², adj-R², F
print(ols.conf_int())                          # confidence intervals for beta
# Robust (heteroscedasticity-consistent) standard errors when variance isn't constant:
ols_robust = sm.OLS(df["price"], X_sm).fit(cov_type="HC3")

When to use which: reach for scikit-learn when the goal is prediction, pipelines, cross-validation, and deployment; reach for statsmodels when the goal is inference — you want the coefficient table with standard errors, $t$-statistics, $p$-values, $R^2$/adjusted $R^2$, the $F$-test, and residual diagnostics.

23. Library Parameters Mapped to Mathematics

sklearn.linear_model.LinearRegression

  • fit_intercept — whether to include $\beta_0$, i.e. prepend the ones-column. If your data is already centered, False skips it. Mathematically toggles whether the column space includes the constant direction.
  • positive — constrains $\hat\beta \ge 0$. This turns the unconstrained normal-equations solve into a constrained quadratic program (no longer closed-form). Use only when negativity is physically meaningless.

sklearn.linear_model.Ridge / RidgeCV

  • alpha (this is $\lambda$) — the coefficient on the $\ell_2$ penalty in $\|y-X\beta\|^2 + \alpha\|\beta\|^2$. Increasing it strengthens the diagonal loading in $(X^\top X + \alpha I)^{-1}$: coefficients shrink toward zero, variance falls, bias rises (§10, §11). alpha=0 recovers OLS. RidgeCV(alphas=...) searches this grid by CV.
  • solver — 'cholesky' solves the normal equations directly (fast, needs well-conditioned $X^\top X + \alpha I$); 'svd' is most stable; 'sag'/'saga' are stochastic solvers for large $n$. These are numerical choices; they compute the same estimator (§5).

sklearn.linear_model.Lasso / ElasticNet

  • alpha — the $\ell_1$ (or combined) penalty strength; larger → more coefficients driven exactly to zero (sparsity from the diamond geometry, §19).
  • l1_ratio (elastic net) — mixes $\ell_1$ and $\ell_2$: 1.0 = pure lasso, 0.0 = pure ridge. Controls the shape of the constraint region, hence whether shrinkage is sparse or smooth.
  • max_iter, tol — because lasso has no closed form, these govern the coordinate-descent optimiser's iterations and convergence tolerance.

statsmodels.OLS(...).fit(cov_type=...)

  • cov_type='HC0'…'HC3' — swaps the naive covariance $\hat\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$ for a heteroscedasticity-robust (sandwich) estimator. Doesn't change $\hat\beta$; changes the standard errors so inference stays valid when homoscedasticity fails (§18).

24. Common Interview Questions

1. Derive the OLS estimator and state when it's unique.
Minimise $\|y-X\beta\|^2$; gradient $-2X^\top(y-X\beta)=0$ gives the normal equations $X^\top X\hat\beta=X^\top y$, so $\hat\beta=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top y$. Unique iff $X$ has full column rank (equivalently $X^\top X$ invertible); otherwise infinitely many minimisers (the min-norm one is $X^+y$ via the pseudoinverse).

2. "Linear regression assumes a linear relationship between $x$ and $y$" — precisely wrong how?
It assumes linearity in the parameters, not the inputs. $y=\beta_0+\beta_1 x+\beta_2 x^2+\varepsilon$ is a linear model. You can fit any curve as long as it's a linear combination of (possibly nonlinear) features.

3. Why divide RSS by $n-p-1$, not $n$, to estimate $\sigma^2$?
The residuals satisfy $p+1$ linear constraints ($X^\top\hat\varepsilon=0$), so they live in an $(n-p-1)$-dimensional space; $\mathbb{E}[\text{RSS}]=(n-p-1)\sigma^2$. Dividing by $n$ gives a biased (too small) estimate.

4. What does Gauss–Markov actually guarantee, and what does it not?
Guarantees OLS is minimum-variance among linear unbiased estimators, under zero-mean, homoscedastic, uncorrelated errors — no normality required. It does not say OLS beats biased estimators (ridge often has lower MSE), nor does it justify $t$-tests/CIs (those need normality or large samples).

5. Explain the difference between a confidence interval and a prediction interval.
CI is for the mean response $x_\ast^\top\beta$: variance $\sigma^2 x_\ast^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}x_\ast$. PI is for a new observation $y_\ast$: adds irreducible noise, variance $\sigma^2(1+x_\ast^\top(X^\top X)^{-1}x_\ast)$ — always wider.

6. Multicollinearity: does it bias coefficients, hurt predictions, or something else?
It does not bias coefficients and often doesn't hurt prediction. It inflates coefficient variance (near-singular $X^\top X$), making estimates unstable and inference unreliable. Fix: drop/combine features, or regularise.

7. Show that maximising the Gaussian likelihood equals minimising RSS.
$\log p(y\mid\beta)= \text{const} - \frac{1}{2\sigma^2}\text{RSS}(\beta)$. The only $\beta$-dependence is $-\text{RSS}/(2\sigma^2)$, so $\arg\max_\beta \ell = \arg\min_\beta \text{RSS}$.

8. Your $R^2$ is 0.95 on train but the model is useless out of sample — how?
Overfitting (too many features / high-degree polynomial, small $n$), leakage, or in-sample $R^2$ measured on training data. $R^2$ always rises with more features; use adjusted $R^2$ and, above all, held-out/CV error.

9. When does OLS fail entirely, and what do you do?
When $p\ge n$ or features are perfectly collinear, $X^\top X$ is singular → no unique inverse. Use ridge (always invertible: $X^\top X+\lambda I$), the pseudoinverse (min-norm solution), or reduce dimensionality (PCA, feature selection).

10. Why is ridge sometimes better than the "optimal" OLS?
Gauss–Markov only optimises within unbiased estimators. Ridge accepts bias to slash variance; when variance dominates the error (correlated/many features), the biased ridge estimate has lower total MSE. This is the bias–variance trade-off made concrete.

11. (Debugging) Your gradient descent for linear regression diverges. Likely causes?
Learning rate too large (need $\eta < 2/\lambda_{\max}(X^\top X)$), or unscaled features making $X^\top X$ badly conditioned so a single $\eta$ can't suit all directions. Standardise features and reduce $\eta$.

12. How would you make linear regression robust to outliers?
Replace squared loss with Huber loss (quadratic near zero, linear in the tails), use RANSAC, or quantile/least-absolute-deviations regression. Squared loss's quadratic penalty is what makes plain OLS fragile.

25. Common Misconceptions

  1. "Linear regression can only fit straight lines." It fits any function linear in the parameters — polynomials, splines, interactions, log terms. The features can be arbitrarily nonlinear transforms of the raw inputs.
  2. "You must standardise features for OLS." Not for the plain closed-form solution — it's invariant to feature rescaling. Standardisation matters for regularised regression, gradient-based fitting, and comparing coefficient magnitudes, not for OLS correctness.
  3. "A high $R^2$ means a good model." $R^2$ measures variance explained in-sample and never decreases with added features. It says nothing about correct functional form, out-of-sample accuracy, or valid inference. Curved residuals with high $R^2$ are common.
  4. "Insignificant $p$-value / small coefficient means the feature is unimportant." Multicollinearity can inflate SEs and shrink individual significance even when the feature matters; scale affects raw coefficient size. Significance is about estimation precision, not importance.
  5. "OLS gives the true causal effect of $x_j$." OLS estimates a conditional-mean association. Causal interpretation requires exogeneity/no confounding — an assumption about the world, not something the regression can supply. Omitted-variable bias silently corrupts the coefficients.
  6. "Confidence and prediction intervals are the same thing." The CI covers the mean response; the PI covers an individual future observation and is strictly wider because it includes irreducible noise.

26(s). Solutions

Level 1

  1. Orthogonality: at the minimum the gradient $-2X^\top(y-X\hat\beta)=0$, i.e. $X^\top\hat\varepsilon=0$ — each column of $X$ dotted with the residual is zero, which is the normal equations. Geometrically, $\hat y$ is the projection of $y$, so the residual is perpendicular to the column space.
  2. Fittable-nonlinear: $y=\beta_0+\beta_1 x+\beta_2 x^2$ (linear in $\beta$). Not fittable directly: $y=\beta_0 e^{\beta_1 x}$ (nonlinear in $\beta$ — needs nonlinear least squares, or linearise via logs if the noise structure allows).
  3. OLS has the smallest variance of all linear unbiased estimators when errors have mean zero, constant variance, and are uncorrelated. Normality is not required.
  4. $R^2=1-\text{RSS}/\text{TSS}$; adding a feature can only shrink RSS (the old fit is still available), so $R^2$ can't fall. Adjusted $R^2$ divides RSS by $n-p-1$, which shrinks as $p$ grows, penalising useless features.
  5. When the loss you care about is proportional to absolute error and outliers shouldn't dominate — e.g. forecasting delivery times where being 60 min late is twice as bad as 30 min, not four times. RMSE would over-weight rare huge errors.

Level 2

  1. $\nabla=-2X^\top y+2X^\top X\beta$; Hessian $=2X^\top X\succeq 0$, and $\succ0$ under full rank, so RSS is strictly convex → the stationary point is the unique global minimum.
  2. Symmetry: $H^\top = X((X^\top X)^{-1})^\top X^\top = H$ (since $X^\top X$ symmetric). Idempotent: $H^2=X(X^\top X)^{-1}(X^\top X)(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top=X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top=H$. Trace: $\operatorname{tr}(X(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top)=\operatorname{tr}((X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top X)=\operatorname{tr}(I_{p+1})=p+1$ (cyclic property).
  3. $\operatorname{Var}(\hat\beta)=\operatorname{Var}((X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top\varepsilon)=(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top(\sigma^2 I)X(X^\top X)^{-1}=\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$ (the outer $(X^\top X)^{-1}X^\top X(X^\top X)^{-1}$ collapses).
  4. As $\lambda\to\infty$, $(X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}\to\frac1\lambda I\to 0$, so $\hat\beta_\lambda\to0$. Variance: $\sigma^2 (X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}X^\top X (X^\top X+\lambda I)^{-1}$; the extra $+\lambda I$ enlarges the inverted matrix's eigenvalue denominators, shrinking every variance term below the OLS value $\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}$.
  5. Means: $\bar x=1.5,\bar y=3$. $S_{xx}=\sum(x-\bar x)^2=2.25+0.25+0.25+2.25=5$; $S_{xy}=\sum(x-\bar x)(y-\bar y)=(-1.5)(-2)+(-0.5)(0)+(0.5)(0)+(1.5)(2)=3+0+0+3=6$. Slope $\hat\beta_1=S_{xy}/S_{xx}=6/5=1.2$; intercept $\hat\beta_0=\bar y-\hat\beta_1\bar x=3-1.8=1.2$. Fitted: $1.2,2.4,3.6,4.8$; residuals: $-0.2,0.6,-0.6,0.2$; RSS $=0.04+0.36+0.36+0.04=0.8$. TSS $=4+0+0+4=8$; $R^2=1-0.8/8=0.9$.

Level 3

  1. Q,R = np.linalg.qr(Xd); beta = np.linalg.solve(R, Q.T @ y). Matches LinearRegression to floating-point tolerance; QR stays stable where forming $X^\top X$ would lose precision on correlated columns.
  2. Expect residuals-vs-fitted curvature if a nonlinear term is missing; funnel shape signals heteroscedasticity; Q–Q tail departures signal outliers/non-normality; leverage plot flags influential points via Cook's distance.
  3. Typical result: OLS has lowest bias but highest CV RMSE when $p\approx n$; ridge/lasso trace a U-shaped CV-RMSE-vs-$\lambda$ curve with a minimum at intermediate $\lambda$ — the bias–variance sweet spot; lasso additionally zeroes out weak features.
  4. A single outlier at high leverage can shift the slope substantially (often tens of percent); Huber refit barely moves because large residuals are penalised only linearly.
  5. Leaking (scaling on full data before split) typically yields a modestly optimistic CV RMSE; the correct in-fold pipeline gives a higher, honest estimate — demonstrating why preprocessing must live inside the CV loop.

27. Summary Cheat Sheet — Linear Regression

Model equation$\hat y = X\beta$, with $y = X\beta + \varepsilon$

Key assumptionsLinearity in params, full rank, $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon\mid X]=0$, homoscedastic, uncorrelated errors; normality only for exact inference
Main guaranteeGauss–Markov: OLS is BLUE
Important hyperparametersNone for OLS; $\lambda$ (ridge/lasso), $\ell_1$ vs $\ell_2$, feature degree

Prediction cost$O(p)$ per point
Main advantagesExact optimum, interpretable, uncertainty quantification, cheap, data-efficient
