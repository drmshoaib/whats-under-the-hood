What's Under the Hood? · #03 · Week 3

Lecture 3: k-Nearest Neighbours

Part I: Mathematical Foundations and Classical Supervised Learning
Dr. Muhammad Shoaib · Applied Mathematics & Data Science · LinkedIn newsletter · every lecture follows the same 27 sections — use the sidebar to navigate.

The first two lectures fitted parameters: we chose a functional form ($x^\top\beta$), estimated coefficients, and then discarded the training data, because prediction needed only $\hat\beta$. k-nearest neighbours (KNN) does the opposite. It fits nothing. It keeps every training example and, when asked to predict, finds the most similar stored examples and copies their answer. This makes KNN the standard example of an instance-based, non-parametric, lazy learner. It also introduces two ideas that recur throughout the course: similarity (distance) can itself be a model, and flexibility without structure degrades quickly as the dimension grows. KNN is where the curse of dimensionality first appears in full, and seeing why it breaks KNN explains why so much of machine learning is about finding good low-dimensional representations.

1. Motivation and Problem Setting

The problem. Given labelled training data $\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$ with $x_i\in\mathbb{R}^p$, predict the label of a new point $x_\ast$. For classification, $y_i$ is a class; for regression, $y_i$ is a real number. KNN answers by finding the $k$ training points closest to $x_\ast$ and aggregating their labels: a majority vote (classification) or an average (regression).

The core idea and why it was developed. KNN formalises the oldest intuition in prediction: things that are similar tend to have similar outcomes. If a new patient's measurements closely match those of past patients who had condition A, predict A. It requires no assumption about the functional form of the relationship. Fix & Hodges introduced the nearest-neighbour rule in 1951 for nonparametric discrimination; Cover and Hart (1967) proved the asymptotic error bound of §4.3, which gave the method a theoretical footing.

Where it sits. KNN is supervised, non-parametric (its complexity grows with the data, not a fixed parameter count), instance-based or memory-based, and lazy (no training-time model fitting; all work is deferred to prediction time). It is a local method: the prediction at $x_\ast$ depends only on a neighbourhood, unlike the global parametric fits of Lecture 1 and Lecture 2. It is also the simplest member of the distance-based family that leads into clustering: K-Means (coming in Week 12) and DBSCAN (coming in Week 13).

Assumptions.

  1. Local smoothness: the target varies smoothly enough that nearby points share labels: $P(y\mid x)$ (or $\mathbb{E}[y\mid x]$) is roughly constant within a small neighbourhood.
  2. A meaningful distance metric: "closeness" in feature space must correspond to similarity in outcome. This is a strong, often overlooked assumption.
  3. Comparable feature scales: because distance mixes features additively, they must be on commensurate scales (otherwise one feature dominates; see §13).
  4. Sufficient density: enough training points that a small neighbourhood actually contains relevant examples. This is what fails in high dimensions.

When reasonable / unreasonable. KNN works well in low-to-moderate dimensions with abundant, well-scaled data and locally smooth targets (e.g. geospatial interpolation, recommendation by similarity, simple image tasks after good feature extraction). It fails when dimensionality is high (distances lose contrast), when features are on very different scales or many are irrelevant, when the data are sparse, or when prediction latency/memory matters (it stores everything).

2. Core Intuition

Imagine plotting your training data as coloured dots (one colour per class). To classify a new grey dot, draw a small circle around it and grow the circle until it captures exactly $k$ coloured dots; whichever colour is the majority inside the circle wins. That is the entire algorithm. For regression, instead of voting you average the numeric labels of those $k$ neighbours.

Why the mechanism works. If the true label surface is locally smooth, then the labels of the closest points are the best available evidence about the label here; no fitted model is needed, because the data are the model. Averaging $k$ of them reduces noise: one mislabelled or noisy neighbour is outvoted. So $k$ is a smoothing dial: small $k$ trusts the very nearest evidence (jagged, low bias, high variance), large $k$ averages over a wide region (smooth, higher bias, lower variance).

Geometric intuition. With $k=1$, the plane is carved into Voronoi cells: each training point owns the region closer to it than to any other, and every query inside that cell inherits that point's label. The decision boundary is the jagged union of cell walls (§7). Larger $k$ smooths these walls.

Probabilistic intuition. KNN classification estimates $P(y=c\mid x_\ast)$ by the local class frequency: the fraction of the $k$ neighbours in class $c$. It is a crude but consistent estimate of the conditional distribution: crude because the neighbourhood is finite, consistent because as $n\to\infty$ and $k\to\infty$ (slowly) the neighbourhood shrinks to $x_\ast$ while containing ever more points.

Tiny numerical seed. Training points on a line: $x=1$ (class A), $x=2$ (A), $x=5$ (B), $x=6$ (B). Query $x_\ast=3$. Distances: $2,1,2,3$. With $k=1$ the nearest is $x=2$ → A. With $k=3$ the three nearest are $x=2,1,5$ (distances $1,2,2$) → votes A,A,B → A. We compute a richer 2D example in §6.

3. Mathematical Formulation

Notation. Training set $\mathcal{D}=\{(x_i,y_i)\}_{i=1}^n$, $x_i\in\mathbb{R}^p$. Query point $x_\ast$. A distance function $d(\cdot,\cdot)$. Let $N_k(x_\ast)$ denote the index set of the $k$ training points with smallest $d(x_\ast,x_i)$ (the $k$ nearest neighbours).

Distance metrics. The Minkowski distance of order $q$ unifies the common choices:

$$ d_q(x,x') = \left(\sum_{j=1}^p |x_j-x'_j|^q\right)^{1/q}. $$
  • $q=2$: Euclidean distance $\sqrt{\sum_j (x_j-x'_j)^2}$, the straight-line distance and the usual default.
  • $q=1$: Manhattan (city-block) distance $\sum_j|x_j-x'_j|$, less sensitive to outliers and often better than Euclidean in higher dimensions (Aggarwal, Hinneburg and Keim, 2001).
  • $q\to\infty$: Chebyshev distance $\max_j|x_j-x'_j|$.

Other useful metrics: cosine distance $1-\frac{x^\top x'}{\|x\|\|x'\|}$ (direction without magnitude, as for text and embeddings), Hamming (categorical), and the Mahalanobis distance $d_M(x,x')=\sqrt{(x-x')^\top\Sigma^{-1}(x-x')}$, which accounts for feature correlations and scale via the covariance $\Sigma$.

Classification rule. Predict the majority class among the neighbours:

$$ \hat y(x_\ast) = \arg\max_{c}\ \sum_{i\in N_k(x_\ast)} \mathbb{1}[y_i=c], $$

and the class-probability estimate $\hat P(y=c\mid x_\ast)=\frac1k\sum_{i\in N_k(x_\ast)}\mathbb{1}[y_i=c]$.

Regression rule. Average the neighbours' targets:

$$ \hat y(x_\ast) = \frac1k\sum_{i\in N_k(x_\ast)} y_i. $$

Distance weighting. Instead of counting all $k$ neighbours equally, weight each by closeness (a common choice $w_i=1/d(x_\ast,x_i)$, or a Gaussian kernel $w_i=e^{-d^2/2h^2}$):

$$ \hat y(x_\ast)=\frac{\sum_{i\in N_k}w_i\,y_i}{\sum_{i\in N_k}w_i}\quad(\text{regression}),\qquad \hat y(x_\ast)=\arg\max_c\sum_{i\in N_k}w_i\,\mathbb{1}[y_i=c]\quad(\text{classification}). $$

Weighting reduces the influence of the more distant neighbours, so a larger $k$ can be used without letting less relevant points dominate. It does not by itself make the prediction continuous in $x_\ast$: with a fixed $k$ the prediction can still jump when one neighbour replaces another at the edge of the neighbourhood, and classification labels remain discrete.

4. Derivation

KNN has no parameters to estimate, so "derivation" means understanding why the rule is statistically sound and what it converges to.

4.1 KNN as a local estimate of the conditional expectation

The optimal regression predictor under squared loss is the conditional mean $f(x)=\mathbb{E}[y\mid x]$; the optimal classifier under 0–1 loss is the Bayes rule $\arg\max_c P(y=c\mid x)$. Both require the unknown conditional distribution at $x_\ast$. KNN approximates it by a local sample average:

$$ \mathbb{E}[y\mid x_\ast]\approx\frac1k\sum_{i\in N_k(x_\ast)}y_i. $$

The justification: if $P(y\mid x)$ is continuous, then for $x$ near $x_\ast$ we have $P(y\mid x)\approx P(y\mid x_\ast)$, so the neighbours are approximately i.i.d. draws from the target distribution at $x_\ast$, and their sample average estimates its mean. It is a Monte Carlo estimate over a shrinking neighbourhood.

4.2 The bias–variance trade-off in $k$ (regression)

Model $y_i=f(x_i)+\varepsilon_i$ with errors that are independent given the training locations $X=(x_1,\ldots,x_n)$, with $\mathbb{E}[\varepsilon_i\mid X]=0$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\varepsilon_i\mid X)=\sigma^2$. The KNN estimate at $x_\ast$ is $\hat f(x_\ast)=\frac1k\sum_{i\in N_k}y_i$. Conditional on $X$, so that the neighbour set $N_k$ is fixed, its variance and bias are

$$ \operatorname{Var}\big(\hat f(x_\ast)\mid X\big)=\frac{\sigma^2}{k}, \qquad \operatorname{Bias}\big(\hat f(x_\ast)\mid X\big)=\frac1k\sum_{i\in N_k}f(x_i)-f(x_\ast). $$

These two expressions contain the whole trade-off:

  • Variance $=\sigma^2/k$: averaging more neighbours cuts variance like any sample mean. Larger $k$ → less variance.
  • Bias: the average of the neighbours' true values minus the true value here. As $k$ grows, the neighbourhood widens to include points where $f$ differs from $f(x_\ast)$, so the bias typically increases, though not necessarily monotonically: it depends on how $f$ varies across the neighbours actually selected.

The optimal $k$ balances $\sigma^2/k$ against the squared bias, giving the same U-shaped total-error curve as every model in this course, now controlled by a single integer. Unconditionally, with random training locations, the variance also contains the variation of the neighbours' function values $f(x_i)$; the conditional expressions above are the ones usually quoted.

4.3 The Cover–Hart result (why even 1-NN is not naive)

Cover and Hart (1967) proved that as $n\to\infty$, the error rate of the 1-nearest-neighbour classifier, $R_{1\text{NN}}$, is bounded by

$$ R^\ast \le R_{1\text{NN}} \le R^\ast\left(2-\frac{M}{M-1}R^\ast\right)\le 2R^\ast, $$

where $R^\ast$ is the (optimal) Bayes error rate and $M$ the number of classes. In words: with infinite data, even the crude single-neighbour rule is never worse than twice the best achievable error. The intuition: as $n\to\infty$ the nearest neighbour of $x_\ast$ converges to $x_\ast$ itself, so it is effectively an independent draw from $P(y\mid x_\ast)$; the chance the query and its neighbour disagree is what produces the factor-of-two bound. The guarantee is asymptotic, which is why finite-sample, high-dimensional KNN can still perform poorly (§4.4).

4.4 The curse of dimensionality (derived)

KNN relies on neighbourhoods being small (so labels are locally constant) yet populated (so averages are stable). In high dimensions these two requirements collide. Consider $n$ points uniform in the unit hypercube $[0,1]^p$. To capture a fraction $r$ of the data in a hypercube neighbourhood, its side length must be

$$ \ell(r)=r^{1/p}. $$

To capture just 1% of the data ($r=0.01$) in $p=10$ dimensions requires $\ell=0.01^{0.1}\approx0.63$, a neighbourhood spanning 63% of the range of every feature. The "local" neighbourhood is no longer local, so the smoothness assumption fails: the neighbours are far away and their labels need not resemble the query's.

Worse, distances concentrate. Under fairly general conditions on the data distribution (Beyer et al., 1999), as $p\to\infty$,

$$ \frac{\max_i d(x_\ast,x_i)-\min_i d(x_\ast,x_i)}{\min_i d(x_\ast,x_i)}\to 0, $$

meaning the nearest and farthest points become nearly equidistant: "nearest neighbour" loses meaning because there is no contrast between near and far. This is the main reason KNN (and distance-based methods generally) degrade in high dimensions, and why dimensionality reduction (coming in Week 14) or learned representations are often prerequisites.

5. Algorithm

Training (trivial, hence "lazy"): store $\mathcal{D}$, optionally build a spatial index (KD-tree / ball-tree) to speed up neighbour search.

Training:
  1. Store all (x_i, y_i).            # O(np) copy of the data; optionally build KD/ball-tree

Prediction for a query x*:
  1. For each training point x_i, compute distance d(x*, x_i).   # brute force: O(np)
     (or query the KD/ball-tree for the k nearest)
  2. Select the k smallest distances → neighbour set N_k(x*).    # O(n log k) with a heap
  3. Classification: return the (optionally distance-weighted) majority vote of {y_i : i ∈ N_k}.
     Regression:     return the (optionally distance-weighted) mean of {y_i : i ∈ N_k}.
  4. For probabilities: return class fractions among the k neighbours.

Inputs: training data, $k$, distance metric (and $q$ for Minkowski), weighting scheme. Initialisation: none (no parameters). Stopping: none (one pass per query). Outputs: predicted label / value / class probabilities.

Ties. In classification, break class ties by the closer aggregate distance or a fixed order; in neighbour-distance ties, include all tied points or break arbitrarily. With uniform weights, choosing odd $k$ avoids two-class vote ties; weighted votes can still tie, though rarely.

Two features, binary classification. Training set:

Query $x_\ast=(3,3)$. Use Euclidean distance.

Step 1: squared distances (compare via squares; monotone, avoids square roots):

$$ d_1^2=(3-1)^2+(3-1)^2=4+4=8,\quad d_2^2=(3-1)^2+(3-2)^2=4+1=5, $$ $$ d_3^2=(3-2)^2+(3-2)^2=1+1=2,\quad d_4^2=(3-4)^2+(3-4)^2=1+1=2, $$ $$ d_5^2=(3-5)^2+(3-4)^2=4+1=5,\quad d_6^2=(3-4)^2+(3-5)^2=1+4=5. $$

Step 2: actual distances: $d_1=2.83,\ d_2=2.24,\ d_3=1.41,\ d_4=1.41,\ d_5=2.24,\ d_6=2.24$.

Step 3: classify with $k=1$. The smallest distance is a tie between point 3 (class A) and point 4 (class B), both at $\sqrt2\approx1.41$. This is the query sitting exactly on the boundary between the clusters, a true tie that must be broken by a rule (e.g. lowest index → A). The example deliberately lands on the boundary to show how sensitive $k=1$ is.

Step 4: classify with $k=3$. The three smallest distances are $d_3=1.41$ (A), $d_4=1.41$ (B), and then a three-way tie at $2.24$ among $d_2$ (A), $d_5$ (B), $d_6$ (B). Taking $d_2$ (lowest index among the tied) gives neighbours {3:A, 4:B, 2:A} → votes A=2, B=1 → A. Taking $d_5$ or $d_6$ instead would give A=1, B=2 → B. The prediction is unstable because the query is equidistant from both clusters: predictions at boundary queries are inherently uncertain.

Step 5: distance-weighted $k=3$ (weights $1/d$). Using neighbours {3:A ($w=1/1.41=0.71$), 4:B ($0.71$), 2:A ($1/2.24=0.45$)}: class A weight $=0.71+0.45=1.15$, class B weight $=0.71$ → A. Weighting favours the closest points, but it has not removed the dependence on the selection tie: had point 5 or 6 been admitted instead of point 2, the weighted vote would be B.

Step 6: regression view. If instead the labels were numbers, say $y=(1,2,2,8,9,8)$, then unweighted $k=3$ prediction using {3,4,2}: $\hat y=(2+8+2)/3=4.0$. Distance-weighted: $\frac{0.71\cdot2+0.71\cdot8+0.45\cdot2}{0.71+0.71+0.45}=\frac{1.42+5.68+0.90}{1.87}=\frac{8.0}{1.87}\approx4.28$.

7. Geometry and Visual Interpretation

Voronoi tessellation ($k=1$). The 1-NN classifier partitions feature space into Voronoi cells: cell $i$ is the set of all points closer to $x_i$ than to any other training point. Each cell is a convex polygon (polytope in higher dimensions) bounded by perpendicular bisectors between neighbouring training points. Every query inside cell $i$ gets label $y_i$. The decision boundary is the union of those cell walls that separate differently-labelled cells: a jagged, piecewise-linear surface that hugs the training points. A good visualisation: coloured points with their Voronoi cells shaded by class, the class-change walls drawn bold.

Effect of $k$. As $k$ increases, the boundary smooths: instead of following individual cell walls it responds to local majorities, ironing out the jaggedness. At $k=1$ the boundary can wrap tightly around a single outlier (an island of the wrong colour); at large $k$ such islands are absorbed. In the limit $k=n$ with uniform weights, every query gets the global majority class, a single flat prediction everywhere (maximum bias); distance-weighted predictions still depend on the query.

Neighbourhood shape depends on the metric. Euclidean neighbourhoods are spheres; Manhattan neighbourhoods are diamonds (rotated squares); Chebyshev are axis-aligned squares; Mahalanobis are ellipsoids oriented by the data covariance. The metric reshapes what "near" means, and hence the boundary.

8. Statistical Interpretation

A non-parametric density-ratio / regression estimator. KNN is closely related to kernel density estimation: instead of a fixed-bandwidth window it uses an adaptive bandwidth (the distance to the $k$-th neighbour), which is small in dense regions and large in sparse ones. The window therefore widens where data are sparse and narrows where they are dense, so every estimate rests on $k$ points.

Consistency. KNN is a universally consistent estimator (Stone, 1977): if $k\to\infty$ and $k/n\to0$ as $n\to\infty$ (the neighbourhood contains ever more points yet shrinks to a point), the KNN classifier's error converges to the Bayes-optimal error $R^\ast$ for essentially any distribution. The conditions encode the bias–variance balance: $k\to\infty$ kills variance, $k/n\to0$ kills bias.

Bias, variance, capacity. KNN's effective number of parameters is often taken as $n/k$: with $k=1$ the model has one "parameter" per data point (maximal capacity, minimal bias, maximal variance); with large $k$ it behaves like a low-capacity, high-bias model. This gives a clean way to compare KNN's flexibility to parametric models.

Uncertainty. The class fractions among neighbours give a rough posterior $\hat P(y=c\mid x_\ast)$. With uniform weights they take only the values $j/k$ (with distance weights, weighted class fractions), and for small $k$ they are noisy. Whether they are well calibrated has to be assessed on held-out data; discreteness alone does not establish miscalibration. KNN offers no standard confidence intervals or hypothesis tests; it is a predictive tool, and in this respect it differs most from Lecture 1 and Lecture 2, where inference was central.

9. Training and Optimisation

There is no optimisation. KNN has no loss to minimise at training time and no parameters to fit; "training" is just storing the data (and optionally indexing it). All computation is deferred to prediction, which is why KNN is called a lazy learner. The consequence: training costs one pass over the data and the model updates trivially (just append new points), but every prediction pays the full search cost.

What is "learned" is chosen, not fitted. The design decisions ($k$, the distance metric, the weighting scheme and any feature scaling or selection) are hyperparameters selected by cross-validation (§10, §15). In practice the "training" of KNN is the hyperparameter search plus the preprocessing pipeline; the model itself is inert.

Speeding up neighbour search. The only computational "optimisation" is making search faster: KD-trees (recursive axis-aligned space partitioning; efficient for low $p$, degrade past $p\approx20$), ball-trees (nested hyperspheres; better for moderate $p$ and non-Euclidean metrics), and approximate nearest neighbours (LSH, HNSW graphs) that trade exactness for speed at scale. These change the runtime, not the predictions (except approximate methods, which may differ slightly).

10. Hyperparameters

$k$, the number of neighbours.

  • Mathematically: controls the size of the averaging neighbourhood; variance scales as $\sigma^2/k$, bias typically grows with the neighbourhood width.
  • Increasing $k$: smoother decision boundary, more bias, less variance; less sensitive to noise and outliers; at $k=n$ with uniform weights predicts the global majority/mean.
  • Decreasing $k$: more flexible, jagged boundary, less bias, more variance; at $k=1$ can fit (and overfit to) individual points and noise.
  • Cost: larger $k$ slightly increases the aggregation work but the dominant search cost is unchanged. Guidance: tune by CV; a common starting heuristic is $k\approx\sqrt n$; with uniform weights, use odd $k$ in binary classification to avoid vote ties.

Distance metric (and Minkowski order $q$). Determines the geometry of "near." Euclidean ($q=2$) is default; Manhattan ($q=1$) is less sensitive to outliers and often better in higher $p$; cosine for direction-dominated data (text/embeddings); Mahalanobis to account for correlation and scale. The metric is a substantive modelling choice: a poorly chosen one makes the neighbours uninformative.

Weighting scheme (uniform vs distance). Uniform counts all $k$ equally; distance-weighting ($w_i=1/d$ or a kernel) lets closer points matter more, reducing bias from including farther neighbours and allowing larger $k$. It reduces sensitivity to distant points but can over-trust a single very close point.

Feature scaling / weighting (effectively a hyperparameter). Because distance sums over features, the relative scaling of features is part of the model. Standardisation is close to mandatory, and learned feature weights or metric learning (e.g. LMNN, NCA) can improve results substantially (§13).

Search algorithm (brute, kd_tree, ball_tree). Affects speed, not predictions (for exact search). Choose by $p$ and metric.

11. Bias–Variance Behaviour

  • Overfitting (high variance): small $k$ (especially $k=1$) makes the prediction hostage to the single nearest point, so noise and outliers create spurious islands in the decision boundary; train error is zero at $k=1$ (each point is its own nearest neighbour) but test error is high. This is the classic overfit signature.
  • Underfitting (high bias): large $k$ averages over regions where the true label changes, blurring real structure; at $k=n$ with uniform weights the model is a constant. Both train and test error are high.
  • Capacity $\approx n/k$: KNN is one of the highest-capacity models available at small $k$, which is why it needs abundant data and careful regularisation via $k$.
  • Data-size sensitivity: KNN is data-hungry: its consistency guarantee is asymptotic, and in finite samples (especially high $p$) neighbourhoods are too sparse to be reliable. More data continues to help, whereas a parametric model's accuracy plateaus, but the required amount grows exponentially in $p$.
  • Effect of irrelevant features: unlike trees, KNN cannot ignore useless features: every feature contributes to the distance, so noise dimensions directly inflate variance and degrade locality. Feature selection is a form of regularisation for KNN.

12. Computational Complexity

Let $n$ = training points, $p$ = features, $k$ = neighbours, $m$ = number of queries.

  • Training: $O(np)$ to read, validate and copy the data (keeping a reference to an existing array can be $O(1)$, but any copy or check touches all $np$ values); $O(np\log n)$ to build a KD/ball-tree. There is no optimisation, so training is cheap by the standards of this course.
  • Prediction (brute force): $O(np)$ per query to compute all distances, plus $O(n\log k)$ to select the $k$ smallest with a heap, giving $O(np+n\log k)$; linear-time selection (np.argpartition) makes it $O(np+n)=O(np)$, the usual summary. For $m$ queries the cost is $O(mnp)$: every prediction scales with the size of the training set, which is the main scalability problem.
  • Prediction (KD/ball-tree): roughly $O(p\log n)$ per query in low dimensions, but degrades towards brute-force $O(np)$ as $p$ grows (trees stop pruning effectively beyond roughly $p\approx20$, another consequence of the curse).
  • Approximate NN (HNSW/LSH): sublinear query time, enabling KNN-style retrieval over millions of vectors (the basis of current vector search and retrieval-augmented generation).
  • Memory: $O(np)$: the entire training set must be kept in memory, unlike parametric models storing only $O(p)$ coefficients. This is often the binding constraint in production.

Practical consequence: KNN is cheap to build but expensive and memory-heavy to serve. For large $n$ or high-throughput inference, use spatial indexes, approximate search, or switch to a parametric model that precompiles the data into a small parameter set.

13. Data Preparation

  • Feature scaling: essential. Because distance adds contributions across features, a feature with a large numeric range dominates. Example: income (0–100000) and age (0–100) in raw units → distance is essentially income-only; age is invisible. Standardise (z-score) or min–max scale so features contribute comparably. Unlike OLS, here scaling changes the predictions themselves. (Contrast: coming in Week 6 decision trees are scale-invariant.)
  • Feature selection / dimensionality reduction: critical. Irrelevant features add noise to every distance and trigger the curse of dimensionality. Remove uninformative features, or project with PCA (coming in Week 14) before applying KNN.
  • Categorical variables. One-hot encode and consider an appropriate metric (Euclidean on one-hot behaves oddly; Hamming or Gower distance may be better for mixed data).
  • Outliers. Affect small-$k$ predictions strongly (an outlier can be someone's nearest neighbour); use larger $k$, distance weighting, or a metric less sensitive to outliers (Manhattan).
  • Missing values. KNN needs complete vectors to compute distances; impute first (KNN itself is often used for imputation).
  • Class imbalance. Majority voting is biased towards the frequent class (it dominates neighbourhoods); use distance weighting, class-balanced voting, or resampling.

Not necessary: no distributional assumptions and no linearity. KNN assumes little about the form of the relationship; its demands concern geometry: scale, dimension and density. Temporal drift is still a problem: if the distribution changes over time, stored neighbours stop being representative of new queries (§15).

14. Evaluation

Use the standard metrics for the task; KNN's outputs are ordinary labels or values.

  • Classification: accuracy, precision/recall/F1 (under imbalance), ROC-AUC / PR-AUC. KNN's probability estimates are coarse (values $j/k$ with uniform weights), so check their calibration on held-out data and calibrate if probabilities matter.
  • Regression: MAE, MSE, RMSE, $R^2$, as in Lecture 1.

Why metrics can mislead for KNN specifically:

  • Train accuracy is meaningless at $k=1$: it is always 100% (each training point is its own nearest neighbour), telling you nothing about generalisation. Always evaluate on held-out data.
  • Coarse probabilities can make log-loss and other calibration-sensitive metrics look bad even when ranking/accuracy is fine.
  • Under imbalance, plain accuracy flatters the majority-vote bias.

15. Validation Strategy

  • $k$-fold / stratified CV (note the two different $k$'s: folds and neighbours) to tune the number of neighbours, the metric, and the weighting jointly. Stratify for classification.
  • Nested CV when reporting generalisation after tuning, to avoid optimistic bias.
  • Grouped CV when records cluster: otherwise a near-duplicate of a test point in the training fold makes KNN look artificially perfect (a leakage risk that is especially severe for memory-based methods).
  • Time-series validation for temporal data: never let future neighbours inform past predictions.

Leakage risks: (1) scaling/imputation must be fit inside each fold (fitting the scaler on all data leaks test statistics, and KNN is highly scale-sensitive). (2) Duplicate or near-duplicate rows across train/test folds let a point find "itself" as a neighbour, inflating performance substantially; deduplicate and use grouped splits.

16. Diagnostics

  • Validation-error-vs-$k$ curve: the primary diagnostic: plot CV error against $k$; expect a U-shape, pick the minimum (or the largest $k$ within one standard error, for a smoother model).
  • Decision-boundary plot (2D): visualise how the boundary jaggedness changes with $k$ to see over/underfitting directly.
  • Distance-distribution check: histogram of nearest-neighbour distances; if the smallest and largest are similar, distances have concentrated → the curse of dimensionality is biting, KNN is unreliable.
  • Confusion matrix / residual plots as for any classifier/regressor.
  • Neighbour inspection: for a suspicious prediction, print the actual $k$ neighbours and their distances. KNN shows exactly why it predicted something: every prediction has concrete exemplars.
  • Feature-scaling audit: verify per-feature variances are comparable post-scaling; a single dominant-variance feature signals a scaling bug.

17. Strengths

  • No training / trivial updates. Fitting is a single $O(np)$ pass with no optimisation; adding new labelled data is just an append, which suits streaming and online settings where the reference set grows.
  • Non-parametric flexibility. Makes no assumption about the functional form; can represent arbitrarily complex decision boundaries given enough data, because the boundary is defined by the data itself.
  • Conceptually simple and transparent. Every prediction is explained by concrete neighbours: "we predicted A because these three most-similar past cases were A", a form of example-based interpretability that many complex models lack.
  • Naturally multi-class and multi-output. Voting/averaging extends to any number of classes or several targets at once with no change.
  • Strong asymptotic guarantees. Universal consistency; even 1-NN is within a factor of two of Bayes error (§4.3).
  • Adaptive resolution. The effective bandwidth (distance to the $k$-th neighbour) shrinks in dense regions and grows in sparse ones, using data efficiently where available.
  • Foundation for retrieval. The same nearest-neighbour machinery powers recommendation, semantic search, and retrieval-augmented generation over embeddings.

18. Weaknesses and Failure Modes

  • Curse of dimensionality. In high $p$, neighbourhoods stop being local and distances lose contrast (§4.4), so predictions become unreliable. Example: raw-pixel image KNN performs poorly; the same data through a learned embedding works; the fix is the representation, and changing $k$ does not help.
  • Expensive, memory-heavy prediction. $O(np)$ per query and $O(np)$ storage; painful for large $n$ or low-latency serving. Example: a 10-million-row reference set makes each brute-force query prohibitively slow.
  • Scale sensitivity. Unscaled features silently break the method (one feature dominates the distance). A common, easily-missed bug.
  • Irrelevant features hurt directly. No built-in feature selection; noise dimensions degrade every distance.
  • Sensitive to local noise at small $k$. A single mislabelled point creates a wrong-class island; outliers become spurious neighbours.
  • Imbalanced classes. Majority voting favours frequent classes that flood neighbourhoods.
  • Coarse probabilities. With uniform weights the outputs are fractions $j/k$; check their calibration on held-out data before relying on them.
  • No model to inspect globally. Unlike regression coefficients or tree splits, there is no compact summary of what drives predictions across the whole space, only per-query explanations.

KNN vs SVM. Both can produce nonlinear boundaries, but KNN does so locally and lazily (store everything, decide at query time) while a kernel SVM does so globally and eagerly (solve a convex problem once, keep only support vectors, predict fast). Once fitted, SVM prediction is much cheaper, and its margin-based objective usually copes better with higher dimensions than raw distances do; KNN needs no training and adapts instantly to new data. Both are scale-sensitive. Rule of thumb: prefer KNN for small, low-dimensional, frequently-updated data; prefer SVM when you need fast prediction in higher dimensions.

KNN vs parametric models (Lecture 1, Lecture 2). Parametric models compress the data into a few coefficients (cheap storage/prediction, strong assumptions, global structure, inference available); KNN keeps the data (no assumptions on form, expensive serving, local structure, no inference). KNN wins when the true relationship is complex and local and data are plentiful in low dimensions; parametric models win when structure is simple, data are scarce, or dimensions are high.

KNN and clustering (coming in Week 12, coming in Week 13). KNN is the supervised face of distance-based learning; K-Means and DBSCAN are its unsupervised cousins, using the same notion of proximity to group rather than to label. The "$k$" in KNN (neighbours) and K-Means (clusters) are unrelated, which is a common source of confusion.

20. When I Should Use It

Use KNN when:

  • Dimensionality is low to moderate and data are plentiful and well scaled.
  • The decision boundary is complex or irregular and you don't want to specify a functional form.
  • You need a fast-to-build baseline or an instantly-updatable model (append new data, no retraining).
  • Example-based explanations are valuable ("here are the most similar past cases").
  • You are doing similarity retrieval or recommendation over embeddings (with an ANN index).
  • The task is naturally local (spatial interpolation, "find similar items").

Avoid KNN when:

  • Dimensionality is high (raw high-dimensional features) and not reduced first; the curse dominates.
  • Prediction latency or memory is constrained and $n$ is large.
  • Many features are irrelevant or on incomparable scales you can't fix.
  • You need calibrated probabilities or statistical inference.
  • Data are scarce (neighbourhoods too sparse) or heavily imbalanced.

21. Python Implementation From Scratch

import numpy as np

class KNNScratch:
    """k-Nearest Neighbours for classification or regression, from NumPy.

    Tie rules (documented so that predict() and predict_proba() agree):
      * equal distances → lowest training index (stable sort);
      * equal class weights → first class in sorted order (classes_).
    With weights="distance", a neighbour at distance 0 takes all the weight
    (and the others none), as scikit-learn does.
    """

    def __init__(self, k=5, task="classification", weights="uniform", p=2):
        self.k = k
        self.task = task
        self.weights = weights      # "uniform" or "distance"
        self.p = p                  # Minkowski order: 1=Manhattan, 2=Euclidean
        self.X_ = None
        self.y_ = None
        self.classes_ = None

    def fit(self, X, y):
        # "Training" = memorise the data: one O(np) copy. This is the entire fit step.
        self.X_ = np.asarray(X, float)
        self.y_ = np.asarray(y)
        if not 1 <= self.k <= len(self.X_):
            raise ValueError(f"need 1 <= k <= n, got k={self.k}, n={len(self.X_)}")
        if self.task == "classification":
            self.classes_ = np.unique(self.y_)
        return self

    def _distances(self, x):
        # Minkowski distance from x to every stored point: (Σ|Δ|^p)^(1/p).
        diff = np.abs(self.X_ - x)                       # broadcasting: (n, p)
        return (diff ** self.p).sum(axis=1) ** (1.0 / self.p)

    def _neighbours(self, x):
        # Indices of the k nearest stored points and their vote weights.
        d = self._distances(x)
        idx = np.argsort(d, kind="stable")[:self.k]     # k smallest; ties → lowest index (§6)
        if self.weights == "distance":
            exact = d[idx] == 0.0
            w = exact.astype(float) if exact.any() else 1.0 / d[idx]
        else:
            w = np.ones(self.k)
        return idx, w

    def predict_proba(self, X):
        # Weighted class fractions among the k neighbours; columns follow classes_.
        X = np.asarray(X, float)
        out = np.empty((len(X), len(self.classes_)))
        for r, x in enumerate(X):
            idx, w = self._neighbours(x)
            out[r] = [w[self.y_[idx] == c].sum() / w.sum() for c in self.classes_]
        return out

    def predict(self, X):
        X = np.asarray(X, float)
        if self.task == "classification":
            # argmax over the same columns as predict_proba; ties → first class
            return self.classes_[np.argmax(self.predict_proba(X), axis=1)]
        out = np.empty(len(X))
        for r, x in enumerate(X):                        # regression: weighted mean
            idx, w = self._neighbours(x)
            out[r] = np.dot(w, self.y_[idx].astype(float)) / w.sum()
        return out
# --- demo on the worked 2D example --------------------------------
X = np.array([[1,1],[1,2],[2,2],[4,4],[5,4],[4,5]], float)
y = np.array(["A","A","A","B","B","B"])
model = KNNScratch(k=3, weights="distance").fit(X, y)
print("prediction at (3,3):", model.predict([[3,3]]))
print("neighbour fractions:", model.predict_proba([[3,3]]))

Correspondence to the maths.

  • fit just stores the arrays: the lazy-learning "no training" of §9.
  • _distances implements the Minkowski metric $d_q$ from §3 via broadcasting; self.p selects Euclidean/Manhattan.
  • np.argsort(d, kind="stable")[:self.k] selects $N_k(x_\ast)$ (§3), breaking distance ties by lowest index as in §6. A full sort is $O(n\log n)$; a heap gives $O(n\log k)$ and np.argpartition gives $O(n)$.
  • predict_proba computes the weighted class fractions of §3 in classes_ order, and predict takes the argmax of the same columns, so the two methods share one tie rule (first class in sorted order). A neighbour at distance zero receives all the weight, matching scikit-learn; without this rule $1/d$ would be undefined.

23. Library Parameters Mapped to Mathematics

sklearn.neighbors.KNeighborsClassifier / KNeighborsRegressor

  • n_neighbors (this is $k$): the neighbourhood size. Directly the bias–variance dial: variance $\propto1/k$, bias typically grows with $k$ (§4.2, §11). Small $k$ = high capacity/overfit; large $k$ = smooth/underfit.
  • weights: 'uniform' gives every neighbour equal vote (the plain rule); 'distance' sets $w_i=1/d_i$ (the weighted formulas of §3), letting closer points dominate and enabling larger $k$ without over-smoothing.
  • p and metric: the Minkowski order and metric of §3: p=2 Euclidean (spherical neighbourhoods), p=1 Manhattan (diamond neighbourhoods, less sensitive to outliers), or a named/custom metric (cosine, Mahalanobis, Hamming). Reshapes what "near" means (§7).
  • algorithm: 'brute', 'kd_tree', 'ball_tree', 'auto': the search strategy (§9, §12). Changes runtime, not predictions (for exact search). KD-tree for low $p$, ball-tree for moderate $p$/other metrics, brute for high $p$ or small $n$.
  • leaf_size: tree leaf granularity: a speed/memory tuning knob for KD/ball-trees; smaller leaves = deeper trees, no effect on predictions.
  • metric_params: extra metric arguments (e.g. {'VI': ...}, the inverse covariance $\Sigma^{-1}$, for Mahalanobis, giving the correlation-aware ellipsoidal neighbourhoods of §7).

24. Common Interview Questions

1. Why is KNN called a "lazy" and "non-parametric" learner?
Lazy: it does no work at training time (just stores data) and defers all computation to prediction. Non-parametric: it has no fixed set of parameters; its effective complexity ($\approx n/k$) grows with the data, so it can represent arbitrarily complex boundaries.

2. How does $k$ affect bias and variance? What happens at the extremes?
Variance $\propto\sigma^2/k$ (larger $k$ → less variance), bias typically grows with $k$ (wider neighbourhoods include dissimilar points). At $k=1$: zero bias in the limit but maximal variance and zero training error (overfit); at $k=n$ with uniform weights: predicts the global majority/mean (maximal bias, minimal variance).

3. Explain the curse of dimensionality for KNN, with a number.
To capture a fraction $r$ of uniform data needs a hypercube of side $r^{1/p}$; for $r=1\%$ and $p=10$ the side is $\approx0.63$, 63% of each feature's range, so "neighbours" aren't local. Distances also concentrate (nearest ≈ farthest), destroying the near/far contrast KNN depends on.

4. Why must you scale features for KNN but not for decision trees?
KNN's distance sums feature differences, so a large-range feature dominates and small-range features become invisible, so scaling changes the predictions. Trees split one feature at a time using thresholds, which are invariant to monotone rescaling, so scaling doesn't affect them.

5. What is the Cover–Hart result and why is it surprising?
As $n\to\infty$, 1-NN error is at most twice the Bayes error. Surprising because the crudest possible rule (copy the single nearest label) is guaranteed within a factor of two of the theoretical optimum: the nearest neighbour becomes an independent draw from $P(y\mid x_\ast)$.

6. Your KNN gets 100% training accuracy but poor test accuracy. Diagnose.
Almost certainly $k=1$ (or very small $k$): each training point is its own nearest neighbour, so training accuracy is trivially perfect, an overfit, high-variance model. Increase $k$ (tune by CV), add distance weighting, and check for unscaled/irrelevant features.

7. How do you choose the distance metric?
By the data's geometry and CV: Euclidean for continuous comparable features, Manhattan for outlier resistance or high $p$, cosine for direction-dominated data (text/embeddings), Mahalanobis for correlated features, Hamming/Gower for categorical/mixed. The metric is a substantive modelling choice, tuned like any hyperparameter.

8. Why is KNN slow at prediction and how do you speed it up?
Each query computes distances to all $n$ points: $O(np)$, and stores all data: $O(np)$ memory. Speed-ups: KD/ball-trees (low/moderate $p$), approximate NN (HNSW/LSH) for large-scale sublinear search, dimensionality reduction, or prototype/condensed-NN methods that keep a subset.

9. Is KNN affected by class imbalance? How do you mitigate it?
Yes: majority voting favours frequent classes that flood neighbourhoods. Mitigate with distance weighting, class-balanced/priors-adjusted voting, resampling, or adjusting the decision rule using class priors.

10. Are KNN's predicted probabilities trustworthy?
Treat them with care. With uniform weights they are coarse fractions $j/k$ (only $k{+}1$ possible values); with distance weights they are weighted class fractions; for small $k$ they are noisy either way. Their calibration has to be checked on held-out data. If you need probabilities, use large $k$ with distance weighting and apply post-hoc calibration, or choose a probabilistic model.

11. (Debugging) Adding more features made KNN worse. Why?
Irrelevant features add noise to every distance and push you into higher dimensions (curse), diluting locality; KNN has no built-in feature selection. Remove uninformative features or reduce dimensionality (PCA); consider metric learning to down-weight noise dimensions.

12. Contrast the "$k$" in KNN with the "k" in K-Means.
Unrelated. In KNN, $k$ is the number of neighbours consulted per prediction (a supervised smoothing parameter). In K-Means, $k$ is the number of clusters (an unsupervised structural choice). Same letter, different meaning.

25. Common Misconceptions

  1. "KNN has no hyperparameters because it doesn't train." It has several important ones ($k$, the metric, the weighting and the implicit feature scaling), all chosen by cross-validation. The absence of fitted parameters is not the absence of design choices.
  2. "Bigger $k$ is always safer." Too-large $k$ over-smooths, averaging across real class boundaries and raising bias; at $k=n$ with uniform weights you predict a constant. There is an optimal intermediate $k$.
  3. "KNN works out of the box on any data." It silently fails on unscaled features, high dimensions, and irrelevant features, none of which raise errors; they degrade accuracy without warning. Preprocessing is decisive.
  4. "$k=1$ overfits, so it's useless." 1-NN has strong asymptotic guarantees (≤ 2× Bayes error) and works well with abundant, low-noise, low-dimensional data; it's high-variance, not worthless.
  5. "KNN's class fractions are proper probabilities." They are valid estimates of the local class fractions, but coarse (quantised to $j/k$ with uniform weights) and noisy for small $k$; their calibration must be checked on held-out data rather than assumed.
  6. "Training is expensive." Training is a single $O(np)$ pass to store the data; it's prediction that is expensive. KNN inverts the usual cost profile.

26(s). Solutions

Level 1

  1. Lazy = no training-time computation, all work deferred to query time; non-parametric = no fixed parameter count, complexity grows with data; instance-based = predictions are computed directly from stored training instances rather than a learned summary.
  2. At $k=1$ the boundary is jagged, wrapping around individual points (including noise/outliers); as $k$ grows it smooths and simplifies; at $k=n$ with uniform weights it vanishes: every query gets the global majority/mean.
  3. KNN sums feature differences into a distance, so a large-range feature dominates and rescaling changes which points are "nearest" (and thus the prediction). Trees split on per-feature thresholds, invariant to monotone rescaling, so scaling has no effect.
  4. (1) Nearest-neighbour distances are nearly all equal (distance concentration); (2) adding more features degrades rather than improves accuracy; also: predictions barely change with $k$, and neighbourhoods span most of each feature's range.
  5. Each training point lies in its own Voronoi cell and is its own nearest neighbour (distance 0), so 1-NN returns each training point's own label, giving zero training error by construction (and hence a useless training metric).

Level 2

  1. $\hat f=\frac1k\sum_{i\in N_k}y_i=\frac1k\sum(f(x_i)+\varepsilon_i)$. Variance, conditional on the training locations: $\operatorname{Var}(\frac1k\sum\varepsilon_i\mid X)=\frac1{k^2}\cdot k\sigma^2=\sigma^2/k$ (errors independent given $X$). Bias: $\frac1k\sum_{i\in N_k}f(x_i)-f(x_\ast)$, the mismatch between the neighbours' true mean and the target value; it typically grows as $k$ widens the neighbourhood, while variance $\sigma^2/k$ shrinks.
  2. Side $=0.1^{1/p}$: $p=1\to0.10$; $p=2\to0.316$; $p=10\to0.794$; $p=50\to0.955$. By $p=10$ a "10% neighbourhood" spans ~79% of every feature, so it is not local; by $p=50$ it's essentially the whole cube. Locality is impossible in high dimensions.
  3. $R^\ast\le R_{1\text{NN}}\le2R^\ast$ (two-class): as $n\to\infty$ the nearest neighbour $\to x_\ast$, becoming an independent label draw from $P(y\mid x_\ast)$; query and neighbour disagree with probability up to $2R^\ast(1-R^\ast)\le2R^\ast$, hence the factor of two.
  4. Distances from $(1,1)$: to $(0,0)$ $\sqrt2\approx1.41$ (A); $(2,0)$ $\sqrt2\approx1.41$ (A); $(0,2)$ $\sqrt2\approx1.41$ (A); $(3,3)$ $\sqrt8\approx2.83$ (B). $k=1$: nearest is a three-way tie among A points → A. $k=3$: the three nearest are all A → A.
  5. For 0/1 vectors, $(x_j-x'_j)^2=|x_j-x'_j|$ (since differences are 0 or 1), so $\sum_j(x_j-x'_j)^2=\sum_j\mathbb{1}[x_j\ne x'_j]$, the number of mismatched coordinates, which is the unnormalised Hamming distance. The normalised Hamming distance divides this count by $p$, and the Euclidean distance is its square root; the three are monotone transformations of one another, so they rank neighbours identically without being numerically equal. One-hot encoding turns a single category mismatch into two mismatched bits, which again preserves the ranking. Mixing scaled continuous and one-hot features needs care (e.g. Gower distance) to balance their contributions.

Level 3

  1. Expected outcome: predictions match scikit-learn exactly given the same metric, $k$ and tie rules (the §21 class is written to do so); replacing np.argsort ($O(n\log n)$) with np.argpartition ($O(n)$ selection) should give a speed-up that grows with $n$, since only the $k$ smallest distances are needed.
  2. Expected pattern: error vs $k$ is U-shaped, high at $k=1$ (variance) and rising again at large $k$ (bias); distance weighting usually flattens the right side, because far points are down-weighted. The optimal $k$ sits at the minimum.
  3. Expected pattern: test accuracy falls as noise features are added, since each inflates distances with irrelevant variation and dilutes locality. The rate of decline depends on $n$ and on the signal strength of the informative features.
  4. Expected pattern: without scaling, the neighbours are chosen almost entirely by the largest-range feature, and accuracy should be worse unless that feature happens to be the informative one; with StandardScaler all features contribute. The size of the gap is data-dependent and is the quantity the exercise asks you to measure.
  5. Expected pattern: ANN retrieval should return the brute-force top-$k$ for most queries (recall@k close to 1) at lower latency, with a speed/recall trade-off controlled by the index parameters (e.g. HNSW ef). Report both recall@k and query time.

Connections to carry forward: KNN introduces distance as a model: the idea that proximity in feature space encodes similarity in outcome. The same idea underlies the distance-based family: K-Means (coming in Week 12) and DBSCAN (coming in Week 13) reuse it for unsupervised clustering; kernel methods and SVMs (coming in Week 5) replace explicit distances with inner products in feature space; and embedding-based approximate nearest-neighbour retrieval, used in recommendation and retrieval-augmented generation, is KNN at industrial scale.
